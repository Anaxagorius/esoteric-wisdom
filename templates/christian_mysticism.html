Christian Mysticism

The contemplative heart of Christianity — the tradition of those who sought not merely belief in God but direct, transformative experience of the Divine. From the Desert Fathers to Meister Eckhart, Julian of Norwich to Jakob Böhme, Christian mysticism is one of the most profound streams of spiritual experience in human history.

What Is Christian Mysticism?

Christian mysticism is the dimension of Christianity concerned with direct personal experience of the divine, beyond the mediation of doctrine, institution, or external authority. The term "mystical" derives from the Greek mystikos — relating to the mystery cults, meaning something secret or hidden. In Christian usage it came to denote the interior, hidden dimension of faith: not what one believes about God, but one's living encounter with God.

Two major theological approaches to this encounter have characterised the tradition:

  • ◈ Apophatic (negative) theology — God is wholly beyond human concepts, images, and language. We approach God by systematically removing all images and ideas — the via negativa. What remains when all finite concepts are stripped away is the divine reality itself. Associated with Pseudo-Dionysius, Meister Eckhart, and the anonymous author of the Cloud of Unknowing.
  • ◈ Cataphatic (positive) theology — we approach God through symbols, images, Scripture, beauty, love, and created things, which serve as genuine mediators of the divine presence. Associated with Bernard of Clairvaux, Francis of Assisi, and Ignatius of Loyola.

The goal of Christian mysticism is described variously as:

  • ✦ Theosis (θέωσις, Eastern Orthodox) — deification or divinisation; genuine participation in the divine nature (cf. 2 Peter 1:4). The human being is not merely forgiven but transformed and divinised through union with God in Christ.
  • ✦ Unio mystica (Western Latin tradition) — mystical union with God, the soul's dissolving into divine love. The goal of the contemplative life as described by Eckhart, Tauler, and the Spanish Carmelites.
  • ✦ Contemplatio — the highest form of prayer, beyond words and images; a simple resting in God's presence. Distinguished from meditation (discursive reflection on Scripture or divine mysteries) by its imageless, receptive simplicity.

A key distinction from many Eastern traditions: Christian mysticism insists on the personal God — union with God is not absorption into an impersonal Absolute but a love relationship that, in most accounts, preserves the distinctiveness of the soul even at its deepest union. However, some mystics — notably Meister Eckhart — push this distinction towards its logical vanishing point.

The Early Mystics

🏜️

The Desert Fathers and Mothers

3rd–5th Century CE · Egypt, Syria, Palestine

The monks and nuns who fled to the Egyptian, Syrian, and Palestinian deserts to pursue God through ascetic practice, prayer, and contemplation, away from the distractions of urban Christian life. Chief among them: Antony of Egypt (c. 251–356 CE), whose life by Athanasius became the model of Christian monasticism; Pachomius, who founded communal monasticism; Macarius the Great, revered for his discernment and prayer; and among the Desert Mothers, Syncletica of Alexandria, whose sayings on humility and the interior life are amongst the most penetrating in the entire tradition. Their collected sayings — the Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers) — are still read as living guides to contemplative practice.

🌑

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite

c. 500 CE · Unknown Location

An anonymous Christian theologian who wrote under the name of Paul's Athenian convert (Acts 17:34) and who systematically combined Christian theology with Neoplatonism in four treatises: The Mystical Theology, The Divine Names, The Celestial Hierarchy, and The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy. Massively influential throughout the Middle Ages — his authority, mistakenly attributed to a first-century figure, was virtually unquestioned. The Mystical Theology — only a few pages long — describes Moses ascending into the "divine darkness," beyond all names and concepts of God, into the silence of unknowing. This text is the fountainhead of the entire Western apophatic mystical tradition.

🪜

John Climacus

c. 579–649 CE · Mount Sinai

Abbot of the monastery of Saint Catherine on Mount Sinai. His masterwork, The Ladder of Divine Ascent, describes a 30-step programme of contemplative ascent — each rung corresponding to a virtue to be cultivated or a vice to be overcome, matching the 30 years of Christ's hidden life before his public ministry. Ascent is both moral and contemplative: purity of heart opens the soul to the gift of contemplation. The Ladder remains the standard spiritual manual for Orthodox monks to this day, read publicly in monasteries throughout Great Lent.

📖

Origen of Alexandria

c. 184–253 CE · Alexandria & Caesarea

The greatest early Christian theologian and biblical exegete. Origen developed the three-fold allegorical interpretation of Scripture — the literal, moral, and spiritual (or mystical) senses — which became standard in Christian theology. His Commentary on the Song of Songs (and Homilies on the same) became the foundation for the entire tradition of Christian mystical interpretation of the Song: the Beloved and the Soul enact the drama of mystical union. Origen's speculative theology (pre-existence of souls, universal salvation) was condemned by the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553 CE), but his exegetical and mystical legacy was immense.

The Medieval Flowering

🎼

Hildegard of Bingen

1098–1179 · Benedictine Abbess

Benedictine abbess of Rupertsberg, visionary, composer, natural scientist, and Doctor of the Church (declared 2012 by Pope Benedict XVI). She received vivid visionary experiences from infancy — the "living light" — which she transcribed in Scivias (Know the Ways), Book of Life's Merits, and Book of Divine Works. She also composed 77 extraordinary pieces of sacred music (Symphonia) and wrote pioneering works on medicine and natural philosophy. Her visions combined liturgical imagery with prophetic social criticism.

💌

Bernard of Clairvaux

1090–1153 · Cistercian Abbot

Cistercian abbot of Clairvaux and one of the most influential churchmen of the 12th century. His 86 Sermons on the Song of Songs — which he was still composing at his death — constitute the greatest meditation on mystical love in the entire Latin tradition. Bernard described the "mystical kiss" — the soul's union with the divine Word in prayer — in language of extraordinary warmth and precision. His cataphatic theology of love profoundly influenced Francis of Assisi, Bonaventure, and the entire affective tradition.

☀️

Francis of Assisi

1181–1226 · Franciscan Founder

Founder of the Franciscan order and one of Christianity's most beloved saints. Francis practised a nature mysticism of extraordinary vitality — all creatures as brothers and sisters in a single great family of God. In 1224 he received the stigmata during a vision on Mount La Verna — the wounds of the crucified Christ appearing in his own flesh, the first recorded case. The Canticle of the Sun — one of the earliest poems in the Italian vernacular — praises God through Brother Sun, Sister Moon, Sister Water, and even "Sister Bodily Death." His poverty, joy, and radical identification with the poorest transformed medieval Christianity.

🌊

Jan van Ruusbroec

1293–1381 · Flemish Mystic

Flemish prior of the Groenendaal monastery near Brussels. The Spiritual Espousals is his masterwork, describing three degrees of mystical life: the active life (moral virtue), the interior life (affective prayer and longing for God), and the contemplative life (union with God). His "flowing and ebbing" model of mystical life — the soul drawn into God and then sent back out into action — profoundly influenced the Devotio Moderna movement, Thomas à Kempis, and through them, much of later Catholic spirituality.

⚡

Meister Eckhart

c. 1260–1328 · Dominican Friar

Dominican friar and the greatest speculative mystic of the Latin West — see the dedicated section below for a full account. Eckhart's teachings on the Godhead beyond God, detachment (Abgeschiedenheit), and the eternal birth of the Word in the soul remain among the most challenging and profound in the entire tradition. Condemned posthumously by Pope John XXII in 1329; rehabilitated by centuries of subsequent study.

💛

Julian of Norwich

c. 1342–1416 · English Anchoress

English anchoress enclosed in a cell beside St Julian's Church, Norwich. Her Revelations of Divine Love (Showings) is the first book written in English by a woman. Julian received sixteen visions during a near-death illness in 1373 and spent the following twenty years reflecting on their meaning before writing the Long Text. Her most famous phrase: "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of things shall be well." The hazelnut vision: God showed her something the size of a hazelnut and said it was all that is made — she marvelled it did not crumble to nothing, and was told: "It lasts and ever shall last, for God loves it."

☁️

The Cloud of Unknowing

c. 1375 · Anonymous English Mystic

An anonymous Middle English mystical treatise, likely written by a contemplative monk in the East Midlands. The author instructs the contemplative to place a "cloud of forgetting" between themselves and all created things (including all thoughts and spiritual consolations) and to reach toward God through a "cloud of unknowing" with nothing but a "sharp dart of longing love." No concept, image, or effort of the mind can penetrate the cloud — only love. A masterpiece of apophatic English mysticism, closely related to the school of Pseudo-Dionysius and Richard Rolle.

📿

Thomas à Kempis

c. 1380–1471 · Augustinian Canon

Augustinian canon of the Congregation of Windesheim in the Low Countries. Author of The Imitation of Christ — the most widely read Christian devotional book after the Bible, translated into more languages than any other Western text. A guide to interior life, simplicity, and union with Christ through humble love and service, rather than theological speculation. Its famous opening: "What doth it profit thee to enter into deep discussion concerning the Holy Trinity, if thou lack humility?" The Devotio Moderna movement from which it sprang transformed lay piety across northern Europe.

⚡

Meister Eckhart — A Deeper Look

c. 1260–1328 · Dominican Friar · Paris, Strasbourg, Cologne

Eckhart von Hochheim, known as Meister Eckhart, is the towering figure of medieval Western mysticism — perhaps the most radical and philosophically penetrating mystical thinker in the entire Christian tradition. Born around 1260 in Hochheim, Thuringia, he entered the Dominican order and rose to high academic and administrative positions: twice Prior Provincial of Saxony, Vicar General of Bohemia, and professor of theology in Paris (the highest academic honour in the medieval world). Yet his legacy rests above all on his vernacular sermons — preached in Middle High German to communities of nuns and laypeople, a remarkable departure from the Latin of academic theology — in which he brought the most radical mystical philosophy to ordinary people.

The Godhead (Gottheit)

Eckhart distinguishes between God (Gott) — the Trinity, God in relation to creation — and the Godhead (Gottheit): the absolute divine ground beyond all trinitarian distinctions, beyond all names and relations. "God is one; the Godhead is beyond oneness." The Godhead is the "silent desert" of pure divinity, without name, without activity, without distinction. It is what Pseudo-Dionysius pointed to with his "divine darkness." This is Eckhart's most distinctive and most controversial teaching.

Abgeschiedenheit (Detachment)

The soul must become completely empty — abgeschieden, detached or "released" — of all creaturely things, desires, attachments, and even spiritual consolations. Even the desire for God, if it is a creaturely desire, must be released. The perfectly detached soul is not seeking union with God; it is not even aware of "union." It is simply empty — and into that emptiness God necessarily pours himself: "God cannot not fill an empty vessel." Eckhart is more radical than most: even good deeds, prayer, and sacraments must not be clung to.

Birth of the Word in the Soul

Eckhart's most celebrated teaching: the eternal generation of the Son (Word) from the Father within the Trinity is not only a metaphysical event in eternity but is happening continuously in every sufficiently purified human soul. "The Father gives birth to his Son in eternity, equal to himself... He gives birth to his Son in the soul in the same way as he gives birth to him in eternity." This is not metaphor but metaphysical reality for Eckhart — the same birth, the same event, taking place in the soul of the person who has achieved genuine detachment.

Why he was condemned: Pope John XXII issued the bull In agro dominico in 1329 — one year after Eckhart's death — condemning 17 propositions from his works as heretical and a further 11 as dangerous but capable of an orthodox interpretation. The charges centred on apparent pantheism (the soul seems to be identified with God), on statements that appeared to dissolve the creaturely distinction between God and the soul, and on phrases that could be read as dismissing external religious practice. Eckhart had submitted to Church authority before his death and protested that he never intended heresy. Whether his submission was sincere, strategic, or both remains debated.

His rehabilitation and legacy: Academic study over the 20th century clarified the Neoplatonic and Scholastic philosophical context of Eckhart's statements, showing that many condemned propositions become orthodox when read within their proper framework. Pope John Paul II quoted Eckhart approvingly; he is now widely proposed for formal rehabilitation. His influence has been vast: the Rhineland mystics (Johannes Tauler, Heinrich Suso), the Friends of God movement, the Theologia Germanica, influence on Hegel and Schopenhauer, the profound engagement of D.T. Suzuki (who saw deep parallels with Zen), Thomas Merton, and the entire movement of contemporary contemplative Christianity. To read Eckhart is to encounter the most radical claim of the Christian mystical tradition: that in the ground of the soul, God and the human being are one.

"The eye through which I see God is the same eye through which God sees me; my eye and God's eye are one eye, one seeing, one knowing, one love." — Meister Eckhart, Sermon 23
🔥

Jakob Böhme — The Cobbler of Görlitz

1575–1624 · Görlitz, Silesia

Jakob Böhme (1575–1624) was born to a Lutheran peasant family in Alt Seidenberg (now Zawidów, Poland) and apprenticed as a shoemaker, spending his entire working life in that trade in the small Silesian town of Görlitz. Yet in 1600, while gazing at a pewter bowl catching the light of the sun, he experienced an illumination that he later described as revealing the hidden ground of all things — the very secret of the universe. He called it the "breakthrough of the divine light through the dark centre," a vision of how love and wrath, light and darkness, are the two poles of a single divine life.

His first major work, Aurora, or the Morning Redness (Morgenröte im Aufgang), written in 1612, circulated in manuscript and was promptly confiscated by the local Lutheran pastor Gregorius Richter, who branded Böhme a heretic and had him banned from writing. Böhme fell silent for seven years, then in the last decade of his life wrote with astonishing productivity — producing the De Signatura Rerum (Signature of All Things), the Mysterium Magnum, The Three Principles of the Divine Being, and dozens of other treatises.

The Unground (Ungrund)

The absolute ground of reality that precedes even God in the sense of the personal God of the Bible. The Ungrund is pure indeterminate will — neither being nor non-being, neither yes nor no — a primordial abyss out of which God emerges to know himself. This concept, which has parallels in Eckhart's "desert of the Godhead" and anticipates Schelling's philosophy of freedom, is Böhme's most original metaphysical contribution. God is not simply eternal but has a history — a process of self-revelation and self-discovery.

God's Self-Knowledge and Creation

God cannot know himself in the Ungrund — pure indeterminate will has nothing to reflect upon. He therefore generates the world as a mirror in which to see himself: creation is God's self-revelation, God's self-knowledge made external. This means creation is not an arbitrary act of an all-sufficient God but a necessary moment in God's own life — a deeply un-orthodox but extraordinarily fertile idea that would powerfully influence Hegel's philosophy of Absolute Spirit.

The Seven Nature Spirits

Böhme describes seven cosmic principles governing all of reality: desire or contraction; motion or expansion; anguish (the turning point of contraction meeting expansion — the moment of crisis); fire (the violent ignition of the first three); light (the fourth principle transformed by the birth of love — fire becoming gentle warmth); sound or differentiation (the expression of the new life); and substance or body (the crystallisation of the whole process into material existence). A remarkable pre-scientific phenomenology of consciousness projected onto the cosmos.

Good and Evil as Polarity

Good and evil are not opposed absolutes (dualism) but poles of a single divine dynamic: the will to contraction, self-enclosure, and domination (the dark fire) and the will to expansion, self-giving, and love (the light fire). Evil arises when a will chooses its own contraction against the whole — a free act of the creature. The spiritual path is the transformation of the dark fire into light — not the elimination of the contractive principle (which is necessary) but its redemption through the acceptance of love.

Böhme's immense influence: William Law translated and commented on Böhme in 18th-century England, bringing him to the Cambridge Platonists and the Anglican mystical tradition. William Blake's mythological cosmology — the "Eternals," the fallen Albion, the Four Zoas, the division of Urizen and Los — is deeply Böhmian in structure and feeling. F.W.J. Schelling explicitly called Böhme the unacknowledged founder of German nature philosophy. The Pietist movement, the Philadelphian Society of Jane Lead in England, and through these, early Methodism and Quakerism all bear Böhme's mark. In the 20th century, his influence on C.G. Jung (the problem of the Shadow and the wholeness of God), on the process theologians, and on contemporary esoteric Christianity has been immense.

"In Yes and No all things consist. The Yes is pure power and life, and is the truth of God or God himself. The No is the opposite of the Yes... and it is the cause of the anguish of the eternal nature." — Jakob Böhme, De Signatura Rerum

Later Mystical Streams

⛰️

Spanish Carmelite Mysticism

Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) — Carmelite nun, reformer of the Order, and Doctor of the Church. The Interior Castle describes the soul as a diamond castle with seven concentric "dwelling places" — the journey of prayer is a journey inward, through progressive deepening of attention and surrender, toward the innermost chamber where God dwells as an eternal light. The Life and The Way of Perfection are equally essential. Teresa was a remarkable administrator who founded 17 reformed Carmelite houses despite formidable ecclesiastical and social opposition.

John of the Cross (1542–1591) — Carmelite friar, poet, and Doctor of the Church. His four major works — The Ascent of Mount Carmel, The Dark Night of the Soul, The Spiritual Canticle, and The Living Flame of Love — constitute the most precise and systematic analysis of the mystical path in the entire tradition. The "Dark Night" is not depression or spiritual failure but God's active purifying work in the soul, stripping away all remaining attachments — first to sensory consolations (the night of sense) and then to spiritual ones (the night of spirit) — to prepare the soul for total union. His poetry, from which the prose commentaries unfold, is among the greatest in the Spanish language.

🕊️

Quietism

Madame Jeanne Guyon (1648–1717) — French mystic and the most famous exponent of Quietism. Her Short and Easy Method of Prayer advocated "prayer of simple regard" — a complete, passive surrender to God's will, abandoning all mental effort and activity in prayer in favour of total receptivity. The book became enormously popular across France and was translated into many languages. She was imprisoned by Louis XIV for five years in the Bastille (1698–1703) for alleged heresy and immoral influence on the court.

Her spiritual director, Archbishop François Fénelon — one of the most admired clerics in France — defended her publicly and was censured and exiled to his diocese by Louis XIV for his pains. Quietism, in its extreme forms, seemed to render moral effort unnecessary (if God is doing everything, what need is there for human striving?). The controversy ended the Quietist movement as an organised force within the French Church, but Guyon's writings continued to influence Pietism, Methodism, and the Quakers.

📿

Pietism

A 17th–18th century Lutheran renewal movement centred on personal piety, intensive Bible study, prayer, small-group fellowship (collegia pietatis), and practical charitable works — responding to what its founders saw as the spiritual deadness of confessional Lutheranism. Founders: Philipp Jakob Spener (Pia Desideria, 1675 — the movement's manifesto) and August Hermann Francke, who built an extraordinary complex of educational and charitable institutions at Halle.

Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf (1700–1760) and the Moravian Brethren (Herrnhuter Brüdergemeine) represent Pietism's most intense and creative flowering — a community centred on the wounds of Christ, constant prayer, communal living, and worldwide mission. John Wesley's transformative "heartwarming" experience at a Moravian meeting on Aldersgate Street in 1738 — the moment that launched the Methodist movement — demonstrates Pietism's reach into the English-speaking world. Johann Sebastian Bach's devotional cantatas are the movement's greatest musical expression.

Christian Mysticism Today

Thomas Merton (1915–1968)

Trappist monk of the Abbey of Gethsemani, Kentucky — the 20th century's most important figure in the renewal of Christian contemplative life. His autobiography The Seven Storey Mountain (1948) was an unexpected bestseller and introduced monastic life to post-war America. New Seeds of Contemplation, The Sign of Jonas, and Conjectures of a Guilty Bystander are essential texts. In his later years Merton engaged deeply with Zen Buddhism (through D.T. Suzuki), Theravada Buddhism, Sufism, and the Hindu tradition, becoming a pioneer of inter-religious dialogue. He died mysteriously in Bangkok in 1968, electrocuted by a faulty electric fan, on a visit to an international conference of contemplatives — a death that has given rise to persistent questions.

Thomas Keating (1923–2018) and Centering Prayer

Cistercian abbot of St Joseph's Abbey, Spencer, Massachusetts, who — with fellow monks William Meninger and Basil Pennington — developed Centering Prayer in the 1970s as a contemporary contemplative practice accessible to laypeople. Drawing explicitly on John of the Cross, Eckhart, and the Cloud of Unknowing, Centering Prayer invites practitioners into a state of simple, receptive attention — two daily sits of 20 minutes each, releasing all thoughts to God. The Contemplative Outreach movement Keating founded now reaches hundreds of thousands worldwide.

Richard Rohr (b. 1943)

Franciscan friar and founder of the Centre for Action and Contemplation in Albuquerque, New Mexico. A prolific and widely read contemporary voice integrating Christian mysticism, Jungian depth psychology, the perennial tradition, and social justice concerns. His The Universal Christ (2019), Immortal Diamond (2013), and Falling Upward (2011) have introduced Christian contemplative ideas to very large audiences outside traditional church settings. His "alternative orthodoxy" — rooting Christian life in contemplative experience rather than doctrinal correctness — is both celebrated and contested.

Bernadette Roberts

Lay Catholic contemplative and former Carmelite nun who wrote controversial accounts of the mystical path going beyond even the experiences described by John of the Cross. Her The Path to No-Self and What is Self? describe a stage of contemplative life beyond union with God — a passage in which the self-with-God (the "unitive state") itself dissolves, leaving only "what remains." Her careful comparison with Buddhist anattā (no-self) and her insistence on remaining within the Catholic framework make her one of the most intellectually serious voices in contemporary Christian contemplative writing.

"The contemplative life is not a life apart; it is the depth dimension of every genuine human life." — Thomas Merton, Contemplation in a World of Action
