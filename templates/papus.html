Papus

Papus — the magical name of Gérard Anaclet Vincent Encausse (1865–1916) — was the most influential French occultist of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Physician, prolific author, founder of the Martinist Order and organiser of the French occult revival, he worked tirelessly to systematise and popularise the Western esoteric tradition across an extraordinary range of subjects: Kabbalah, Tarot, astrology, numerology, alchemy, Martinism, and ceremonial magic.

Life & Occult Career

Gérard Encausse was born in La Coruña, Spain, in 1865, to a Spanish mother and a French father who was a chemist. The family moved to Paris when he was four, and it was in Paris that he received his education and spent most of his life. He studied medicine — graduating as a doctor in 1894 — and maintained a medical practice throughout his occult career, combining scientific training with esoteric conviction in a manner characteristic of many fin-de-siècle French occultists.

His esoteric career began in his early twenties when he joined the Theosophical Society in 1882 and immersed himself in the occult section of the Bibliothèque nationale, working systematically through its collection of alchemical, Kabbalistic, and magical manuscripts. He adopted the magical name "Papus" — taken from the Nuctemeron attributed to Apollonius of Tyana, where it designates the physician of the Goetia — and published his first major work, Traité élémentaire de science occulte (Elementary Treatise on Occult Science), in 1888, the same year as the founding of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn.

In 1888, Papus also founded the Groupe Indépendant d'études ésotériques (Independent Group for Esoteric Studies), which became the organisational nucleus of the French occult revival, and co-founded the journal L'Initiation, which he edited until his death and which became the principal organ of French esotericism, publishing contributions from Stanislas de Guaïta, Joséphin Péladan, Sâr Mérodack, and many others.

The Martinist Order

Papus's most enduring organisational achievement was the reconstitution of the Martinist Order in 1891. Martinism — named after the 18th-century French mystic Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin (1743–1803) — had its roots in the theurgical and initiatic work of Martines de Pasqually (c. 1727–1774), founder of the Élus Coëns (Elected Priests), a high-degree Masonic order devoted to the re-integration of humanity with the divine through ritual magic. Saint-Martin had been a student of Pasqually before breaking with ritual magic in favour of the "interior way" of mystical prayer and contemplation — his path of the "Unknown Philosopher" (philosophe inconnu) — and it was this mystical, Christian-Kabbalistic tradition that Papus sought to revive.

Papus claimed to have received transmission of the Martinist tradition through Henri Viscount Delaage and through Augustin Chaboseau, whose family he believed had preserved the Martinist chain of initiation through the 19th century. The reconstituted Martinist Order operated with three degrees of initiation and emphasised the Kabbalistic and theurgical dimensions of the tradition — the ideal of the "reintegration" of humanity with its divine source. Under Papus's leadership the Order spread rapidly across France, then across Europe and into Russia, where Papus himself travelled to the court of Tsar Nicholas II and conducted séances and consultations for the imperial family.

Tarot Scholarship & Kabbalistic Synthesis

Papus's Le Tarot des Bohémiens (The Tarot of the Bohemians, 1889) — translated into English by A. P. Morton in 1892 — was one of the most systematic attempts to present the Tarot as a complete system of occult philosophy. Drawing on Lévi's identification of the Major Arcana with the Hebrew alphabet and extending it to the Minor Arcana, Papus presented the entire Tarot as an encoded summary of the Kabbalah, a "primitive book" containing the whole esoteric wisdom of the ancient world in symbolic form.

His Traité méthodique de magie pratique (Methodical Treatise on Practical Magic, 1891) and La Kabbale (1903) further elaborated his synthetic approach, treating Kabbalah as the universal key to all esoteric traditions — the skeleton on which all the various occult sciences could be hung and from which they derived their deeper coherence. While Papus's scholarship was sometimes superficial and his synthesis eclectic to the point of inconsistency, his extraordinary productivity — over 160 published works — ensured that his influence was vast and widely diffused.

Legacy

Papus died in Paris in October 1916, victim of tuberculosis contracted while serving as a military physician on the Western Front during the First World War. He was fifty-one years old. His death robbed the French occult movement of its most energetic organiser and publicist, and the Martinist Order fragmented in the years following his death into several competing lineages.

His lasting contribution to the Western esoteric tradition lies less in original intellectual achievement than in the extraordinary work of synthesis, popularisation, and organisation. He introduced a generation of readers to the Kabbalah, Tarot, alchemy, and ceremonial magic through works written in a clear, accessible style that made the Western esoteric heritage available to educated non-specialists for the first time. The Martinist Order that he reconstituted continues in several lineages to the present day, maintaining the initiatic chain of the tradition he so energetically revived.

Among the specific legacies of Papus, his identification of the Tarot as a Kabbalistic system remains influential in French and Italian occultism, providing an alternative reading to the dominant Rider-Waite-Smith tradition of the English-speaking world. His connections with the Russian imperial court — and the suggestion that he conducted a successful necromantic séance at which the shade of Tsar Alexander III appeared — have given him a place in the romantic mythology of fin-de-siècle European occultism that he would certainly have relished.

"Occultism is the science of the hidden forces of nature." — Papus
