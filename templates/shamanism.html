Shamanism

The world's oldest spiritual technology — healing through trance, vision, and the timeless conversation between human souls and the invisible world.

✦ What Is Shamanism?

Shamanism is not a religion with a fixed doctrine — it is a set of techniques and a worldview found independently across Siberia, the Americas, Africa, Australia, and Southeast Asia. Scholars estimate its roots stretch back at least 30,000–40,000 years, making it arguably humanity's oldest form of spiritual and healing practice.

The word shaman comes from the Evenki language of Siberia — šaman — meaning "one who knows" or "one who is excited, moved, raised." Anthropologist Mircea Eliade, in his landmark 1951 work Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy, identified the core defining feature: the deliberate induction of an altered state of consciousness — trance or ecstasy — to journey into non-ordinary reality on behalf of the community.

The shaman is simultaneously healer, priest, psychopomp (guide of souls), mediator, storyteller, and keeper of ecological knowledge. They walk between worlds — the ordinary reality of waking life and the invisible landscapes of the upper world (celestial spirits), the lower world (ancestors and animal teachers), and the middle world (the spirit layer behind everyday existence).

The primary tool of the shaman is trance induction, typically achieved through rhythmic drumming (usually around 4–7 beats per second, corresponding to theta brainwave frequencies), rattling, chanting, dancing, fasting, or plant medicines. In this altered state, the shaman's consciousness — often conceived as a free soul — departs the body and travels through layered cosmological realms.

Each culture expresses the shamanic worldview differently, yet certain elements appear with remarkable consistency: a World Tree or World Axis (axis mundi) connecting the worlds; animal spirit helpers or allies; initiation through death-and-rebirth; the retrieval of lost or stolen souls; and a deep, animistic understanding of nature as alive, intelligent, and communicative.

Importantly, shamanism is not about personal enlightenment in isolation — it is fundamentally a communal practice. The shaman suffers, journeys, and returns for the benefit of the tribe. The healed relationship is always between the individual, the community, and the living world.

🌀 The Three Worlds

⬆ Upper World

Realm of sky spirits, celestial deities, and ascended teachers. Often accessed by climbing the World Tree or riding a spirit bird. Associated with inspiration, cosmic law, and high wisdom.

◉ Middle World

The spirit dimension of everyday reality — nature spirits, place spirits, the souls of the living and recently deceased. The shaman works here to heal lost souls, negotiate with land spirits, and divine hidden causes.

⬇ Lower World

Accessed by descending through tunnels, roots, or bodies of water. Home of the ancestors, power animals, and primal earth energies. Rich with raw vitality and deep healing potential. Not a place of punishment — a world of roots.

✦ A History Written in Bone and Fire

The earliest archaeological evidence for shamanic-type practice includes the Lascaux cave paintings of France (c. 17,000 BCE), which many scholars interpret as trance-vision imagery — the therianthropic (half-human, half-animal) figures, the birdman with erect phallus, and the deep rhythmic layering of images suggest altered-state cosmological narratives rather than mere hunting records.

The Shanidar Cave burial (c. 60,000 BCE) in present-day Iraq showed a Neanderthal buried with flowers and ochre — indicating ritual treatment of death predating modern Homo sapiens. While not definitively shamanic, it points to the deep antiquity of spiritual care for the dead.

In 2008, archaeologists excavated the Hilazon Tachtit burial in Israel (c. 12,000 BCE): a woman buried with 50 tortoise shells, a human foot, eagle talons, a leopard pelvis, and other unusual items — almost certainly the grave of a shaman, the oldest yet identified with high confidence.

Siberian shamanism is the form most intensively documented by anthropologists. Among peoples such as the Evenki, Buryat, Yakut (Sakha), and Tungus, shamans were recognised by signs at birth (extra bones, unusual marks, surviving illness), trained through a spiritual death-and-rebirth ordeal, and equipped with iron-hung costumes representing their cosmological armour and spirit helpers.

In the Americas, shamanic traditions range from the ayahuasca-using healers (curanderos) of the Amazon to the vision-quest traditions of the Plains peoples, the Mide (Grand Medicine Society) of the Ojibwe, and the elaborate masked ceremonials of the Northwest Coast nations. Each system is distinct and deeply culturally embedded, yet all share the signature of mediating between human communities and the invisible world for healing and guidance.

Korean mudang (shamans, predominantly women) are among the world's most unbroken living shamanic lineages. Their gut ceremonies — elaborate multi-day rituals involving possession, song, and dance — address ancestral grievances, heal illness, and divine the future. Modern Seoul still contains hundreds of active mudang.

The 20th century brought both near-extinction and renaissance. Colonial suppression, Soviet persecution of Siberian shamans, and missionary activity devastated many traditions. Yet simultaneously, anthropologists like Michael Harner studied Amazonian and other traditions and distilled what he termed core shamanism — a set of cross-cultural practices accessible to modern Westerners, founded in 1980 with the Foundation for Shamanic Studies.

Today, neo-shamanism is a global phenomenon. At the same time, indigenous scholars and practitioners caution against decontextualised appropriation — the extraction of practices from living cultures without respect, reciprocity, or understanding of their sacred context.

Key Moments in Shamanic History

~40,000 BCE

Earliest cave art in Europe and Southeast Asia; therianthropic figures suggest trance-vision cosmologies.

~12,000 BCE

Hilazon Tachtit burial (Israel) — oldest identified shaman's grave, showing elaborate ritual interment.

~3,000 BCE

Bronze Age rock art across Scandinavia and Siberia depicts shamanic journeys, world trees, and spirit boats.

17th–18th century CE

First systematic Western descriptions of Siberian shamanism by Russian explorers and scholars.

1951

Mircea Eliade's Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy brings cross-cultural shamanic scholarship to the world.

1980–present

Michael Harner founds the Foundation for Shamanic Studies; neo-shamanism spreads globally while indigenous revival movements reclaim living traditions.

✦ Symbols, Tools & Sacred Practices

🥁

The Shamanic Drum

The drum is the shaman's horse, their vehicle for non-ordinary travel. Typically made from a single hide stretched over a wooden frame, the drum is considered alive and is given a name. The steady monotonous beat (4–7 Hz) entrains the brain toward theta states — the same frequencies associated with hypnagogic imagery, deep meditation, and REM dreaming.

🦅

Power Animals & Spirit Allies

Each practitioner is accompanied by one or more power animals — non-human spirit beings who offer protection, guidance, and power. Common power animals include eagle (vision, sky travel), bear (healing, introspection), wolf (pathfinding), serpent (transformation, lower world), and jaguar (shamanic sight). The power animal is not a symbol but an actual relationship.

🌀

The World Tree (Axis Mundi)

Found in virtually every shamanic culture: a great tree, pole, mountain, or column of smoke that connects the three worlds. Norse shamans knew it as Yggdrasil; Siberian shamans climbed birch trees notched with steps; Mayan cosmology centred on the sacred ceiba. The axis mundi is the path the shaman's soul travels during trance.

🔥

Sacred Fire

Fire is the living altar of shamanism — the mouth of the spirit world, the transmitter of prayers to the invisible. Ceremonies nearly always include fire. In Mongolian shamanism, the fire spirit Golomt is a primary deity. The smoke carries prayers upward; the embers hold messages. Tending the community fire was a sacred duty.

🪶

The Shamanic Costume

Among Siberian and Central Asian shamans, the ritual costume is a cosmological map made wearable. Iron plates represent spirit armour; bird feathers enable spirit flight; antlers invoke the cosmic deer; fringes symbolise the multiple souls of the shaman. Dressing in the costume is itself a ritual act of transformation — the shaman becomes the centre of all worlds.

🌿

Plant Teachers

Many traditions use plant medicines as portals — ayahuasca (DMT + MAOIs) in Amazonia, peyote among the Huichol, iboga in Central Africa, psilocybin mushrooms in Mesoamerica, amanita muscaria in Siberia. These are not recreational substances but teachers approached with ceremony, intention, and preparation. The plant itself is considered a conscious being.

Universal Shamanic Symbols

  • Spiral — the journey inward and outward; the trance vortex; found in rock art worldwide near sites of ritual use.
  • Serpent / Dragon — the energy of the earth, kundalini-like primal force, guardian of the lower world and healing knowledge.
  • Bird — the soul's vehicle for sky travel; eagle, raven, and owl appear across cultures as shamanic messengers.
  • Labyrinth / Maze — the path of ritual descent; navigating the spirit world; transformational passage.
  • Web / Net — the interconnectedness of all beings; many cultures image the spirit world as a living web or net of relationships.
  • Circle / Wheel — the medicine wheel (Plains traditions), the shamanic drum's circular face, the cyclical nature of time.
  • Half-human / Half-animal (Therianthrope) — the shaman in trance, merged with their animal helper; the oldest recurring image in world art.
  • Rainbow Bridge — the luminous pathway between worlds; appearing in Norse (Bifröst), Australian Aboriginal, and South American traditions.

✦ Societies, Lineages & Hidden Orders

Shamanism is largely a transmitted, lineage-based practice — knowledge passed through apprenticeship, initiation, and direct spirit instruction. Several formal societies and secret orders have preserved or drawn from these traditions.

Known Orders & Societies

Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society)

A healing and ceremonial society among the Ojibwe, Potawatomi, and Menominee of the Great Lakes. Members advance through four (or more) degrees of initiation, each requiring years of learning sacred birchbark scrolls, herbal medicine, and ceremonial protocol. Knowledge is earned, not bought. The Mide is one of North America's oldest documented esoteric healing orders.

Bon (Tibetan Shamanism / Pre-Buddhist)

The indigenous shamanic tradition of Tibet, predating Buddhism by centuries. Bon priests (bonpo) performed elaborate spirit ceremonies, divination, and soul-retrieval. While Bon later developed a monastic and philosophically sophisticated form, its shamanic roots persist — and many Bon and Tibetan Buddhist practices (sky burial, oracle traditions, terma treasure texts) carry shamanic fingerprints.

Umbanda & Candomblé

Afro-Brazilian traditions blending West African spirit-possession practices (Yoruba oricha traditions) with indigenous Tupi shamanism and Catholic imagery. Practitioners become vessels for spirits (orixás, caboclos, pretos velhos) through trance-possession — a living shamanic lineage of millions across Brazil and beyond.

Speculated & Esoteric Connections

The Berserkers & Úlfhéðnar

Norse warrior-shamans who entered battle in states of ecstatic trance, wearing bear or wolf skins and identifying with their totem animal. The bear-shirt (berserkr) and wolf-coat (úlfhéðinn) warriors operated outside normal social rules — initiates of Odin functioning as shamanic warriors. Scholars debate how much of their frenzy was chemically induced, ritualistic, or psychological — likely all three.

Shamanism & the Eleusinian Mysteries

The ancient Greek Eleusinian Mysteries — the most celebrated initiatory rites of antiquity — centred on a secret rite involving the drinking of kykeon, a psychoactive barley drink possibly containing ergot (a natural precursor to LSD). The 2021 book The Road to Eleusis by Wasson, Hofmann, and Ruck argues the Mysteries were fundamentally a shamanic psychedelic initiation — hidden in plain sight for 2,000 years.

Foundation for Shamanic Studies (Modern)

Founded by anthropologist Michael Harner in 1979–80, the FSS codified core shamanism — stripping shamanic techniques of their specific cultural contexts to create a teachable, cross-cultural system. While debated by indigenous critics, it has trained tens of thousands of practitioners globally and functions as the closest thing to a modern shamanic school in the West.

✦ Shamanism in Modern Times

Shamanism is one of the fastest-growing areas of contemporary spiritual practice. Modern practitioners draw from core shamanic techniques, indigenous teachings (where respectful access exists), and personal experimentation. These practices have found applications in psychotherapy, palliative care, trauma recovery, and ecological activism.

Accessible Modern Practices

  • 🥁
    Drum Journeying — using a steady rhythmic drum recording (or a live drum), lie comfortably, set an intention, and allow your imagination to take you into a visualised landscape. The lower world is typically entered via a natural opening in the earth (tree roots, cave, spring). Meet whatever comes with curiosity rather than fear.
  • 🦅
    Power Animal Retrieval — journey to the lower world with the intention of meeting a power animal. Accept whichever animal appears three times or stays with you. Work with it through meditation, dreams, and study of its qualities. The relationship deepens over time.
  • 🍃
    Nature Communion — spend deliberate time with trees, stones, or water bodies, speaking to them internally and listening for responses in your feelings, images, and random thoughts. Animism begins with practicing the perception that everything is alive.
  • 💀
    Ancestor Work — shamanism across cultures includes dialogue with ancestors. Set up a small ancestor altar with photos, objects, and offerings. Speak to them. Notice what feelings, memories, and insights arise. This practice, documented in many psychotherapy traditions, is profoundly healing for family systems.

Shamanism in Healing & Therapy

Soul Retrieval & Dissociation

The shamanic concept of soul loss — a part of the self fragmenting away after trauma — maps remarkably closely onto dissociation in modern psychology. Shamanic soul retrieval ceremonies, in which the practitioner journeys to locate and return lost soul parts, are increasingly recognised as complementary to trauma therapy.

Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

Clinical trials at Johns Hopkins, NYU, and MAPS have demonstrated significant therapeutic effects of psilocybin, MDMA, and ketamine for depression, PTSD, and end-of-life anxiety — effects many researchers describe as functionally shamanic: confronting death, meeting symbolic beings, receiving teachings. The set-and-setting protocol is essentially a medicalised shamanic container.

Ecological Shamanism

Practitioners like Martin Prechtel, Robin Wall Kimmerer, and Joanna Macy have developed frameworks for shamanic relationship with the land as a response to ecological crisis — restoring the animistic perception of a living, grieving world that demands reciprocity rather than extraction.
