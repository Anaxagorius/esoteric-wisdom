Leonard Stringfield

"The Crash Retrieval Archivist" · 1920–1994

Brief Biography

Leonard Harry Stringfield was born on 1 February 1920 in Norwood, Ohio. He served as a U.S. Army Air Forces intelligence officer in World War II, and while returning from Japan in a transport aircraft in 1945, he and the crew reportedly observed three bright objects that caused one of the plane's engines to fail before the objects departed. This early personal experience shaped his subsequent decades of investigation. He founded Civilian Research, Interplanetary Flying Objects (CRIFO) in 1954 and published the newsletter ORBIT, which became one of the most widely read UFO publications in America during the 1950s. He served briefly as an Air Force Ground Observer Corps intelligence liaison. He died on 18 December 1994.

His major life's work — the systematic collection and documentation of what he termed "UFO crash/retrieval" cases — produced seven monographs published between 1978 and 1994, constituting the most comprehensive archive of first-hand witness accounts of alleged crashed craft and non-human bodies assembled before the Internet age.

The Crash Retrieval Monographs

Stringfield's seven crash-retrieval monographs (1978–1994) catalogued accounts from military personnel, physicians, mortuary workers, and others who claimed direct or indirect involvement in the recovery of crashed non-human craft and bodies. His methodology was careful and sober: he interviewed witnesses personally, cross-referenced accounts, withheld identifying information for those who required anonymity, and consistently presented the material as testimony requiring evaluation rather than confirmed fact.

The witness accounts he documented described recurring physical characteristics: bodies in the 3.5–4 foot height range, large heads, oversized dark eyes, minimal mouth and ears, four or five fingers, grey or brown skin — descriptions strikingly consistent across witnesses who had no documented contact with each other and in some cases pre-dated the widespread publication of the "grey" morphology. He also documented medical and physiological details provided by physicians and pathologists who claimed to have conducted autopsies on non-human remains.

Methodological Legacy

Stringfield's work was largely ignored by mainstream science and media but became the foundation for subsequent crash-retrieval research, directly influencing researchers including Stanton Friedman (Roswell investigation), Timothy Good, and eventually the military whistleblowers who came forward in the 2020s. The specific, anatomically detailed witness accounts he documented — from sources including military physicians who were willing to risk court martial to share them — represent an evidentiary archive whose significance has only grown with the passage of time and the emergence of corroborating testimony from current insiders.
