Plotinus

Plotinus — the Egyptian-born philosopher who settled in Rome and became the supreme systematiser of Platonic philosophy — is universally regarded as the founder of Neoplatonism, the metaphysical and mystical tradition that exercised perhaps the single greatest influence on Western religious thought between the 3rd and 17th centuries. His vision of the One as the ineffable source of all being, and of the soul's burning desire to return to that source, gave Christian mysticism, Islamic philosophy, and the Renaissance Hermetic tradition their deepest philosophical foundations.

Life & Background

Plotinus was born c. 204/5 CE, almost certainly in Egypt — Porphyry, his most intimate student and biographer, tells us the philosopher was reluctant to disclose even the city or the day of his birth, as if personal biographical details were a distraction from the philosophical work that truly mattered. This reticence about his own history is characteristic of a man who described himself as "ashamed of being in a body" and who directed all his intellectual and spiritual energies toward the supersession of individual particularity in the universal. What we know of his early life comes primarily from Porphyry's short but invaluable Life of Plotinus, which serves as the introduction to his edition of Plotinus's works. After various early studies, Plotinus arrived at Alexandria c. 232 CE and attached himself to the philosopher Ammonius Saccas — a figure of great importance who also taught Origen the Christian theologian — under whose guidance he studied for eleven years with mounting intellectual satisfaction, declaring at their first meeting that this was the teacher he had been seeking.

In 242 CE, Plotinus joined the military expedition of the Emperor Gordian III against Persia, attracted by the prospect of direct encounter with Persian and Indian philosophy. The expedition ended disastrously with the murder of Gordian, and Plotinus escaped with difficulty to Antioch before making his way to Rome c. 244 CE, where he would spend the remainder of his active philosophical life. In Rome, he established a school that attracted a wide and distinguished circle: his student and editor Porphyry, the philosopher Amelius, the physician Eustochius who attended him in his final illness, and a number of Roman senators, their wives, and other members of the educated aristocracy. So great was his moral authority that many members of this circle entrusted him with their estates and the guardianship of their children. The Emperor Gallienus and his wife Salonina regarded him with particular favour, and Plotinus famously proposed to Gallienus the establishment of "Platonopolis" — a city in Campania to be governed according to the principles of Plato's Laws — a project that came to nothing, apparently due to the opposition of courtiers.

Porphyry records that during the six years they spent together, Plotinus attained four experiences of mystical union with the One — a fact that Porphyry considered remarkable enough to record with precision and that gives the Enneads their distinctive character as the work of a practising mystic rather than a merely theoretical philosopher. In his final years, afflicted by a painful disease that his biographer describes as causing the rotting of his extremities, Plotinus withdrew to the estate of his friend Zethus in Campania, where he died in 270 CE. His last recorded words, addressed to Eustochius who arrived just in time, were: "I was waiting for you before the divine in me departs to unite with the divine in all things." These words express with extraordinary economy the entire philosophical and mystical programme of the Enneads: the recognition of the divine within the individual soul, and the longing for its reunion with the universal divine source from which it came.

The Three Hypostases

The metaphysical architecture of Plotinus's philosophy rests on three fundamental principles or hypostases — the One, the Intellect (Nous), and the Soul (Psyche) — which constitute a hierarchical procession from the absolutely simple and perfect to the increasingly complex, differentiated, and fragmented. This triadic structure is not an arbitrary philosophical construction but reflects Plotinus's sustained attempt to think through the ultimate implications of Platonic philosophy: if the Good or the One is, as Plato argued in the Republic, "beyond being" and the source of all that is, then how does the multiplicity of the world arise from this absolute unity, and how does the soul find its way back to it? The three hypostases are Plotinus's answer to this question, and they constitute one of the most comprehensive and coherent metaphysical systems in the history of Western philosophy.

The One (to Hen) is absolutely beyond being, thought, and description. It is not a being among beings — not even the highest or greatest being — but the source and condition of all being, prior to any distinction between being and non-being. It cannot even be said to be self-conscious, because self-consciousness would imply a duality between a knowing subject and a known object, and the One admits of no such duality. It is perfect and therefore, paradoxically, necessarily "overflows" — not from any deficiency or desire but from the sheer inexhaustible fullness of its perfection. This overflow, which Plotinus calls prohodos (procession), generates the second hypostasis, the Intellect (Nous), by a process analogous to the radiation of light from the sun: the sun does not choose to radiate and is not diminished by radiation; radiation is simply what the sun does by virtue of what it is. The Intellect is the first being — the realm of the Platonic Forms or Ideas — in which thinking subject and thought object are identical: the Intellect knows itself by being the Ideas, and the Ideas are the Intellect's self-knowledge. This is the realm of genuine being, beauty, and truth.

From the Intellect, in a similar act of necessary overflow, proceeds the Soul (Psyche) — the third hypostasis and the principle of life, movement, and temporal existence. The Soul contemplates the Intellect above it and thereby generates below it the physical world, which is the Soul's external expression and "image." Within the overall Soul there are distinctions: the World Soul, which animates and governs the cosmos as a whole; and individual souls, each of which is a partial expression of the Soul principle. Matter (hyle) — the last trace of the One's emanation before it vanishes into absolute nothingness — is the lowest level of reality, described by Plotinus as sheer privation: not evil in itself but the condition of maximum distance from the Good. What is crucial to grasp about this hierarchy is its dynamic, living character. It is not a static chain of beings arranged in descending order but a ceaseless pulsation of procession (from the One downwards through Intellect to Soul and matter) and return (from Soul and Intellect back toward the One in an eternal yearning). The whole of reality is, for Plotinus, a vast living process of self-expression and self-return.

The Mystical Ascent

The soul's journey back to the One is the central practical and spiritual concern of the Enneads — not merely a theoretical consequence of the metaphysical system but the living purpose toward which all of Plotinus's philosophy is directed. The ascent has three broad stages. The first is the awakening through beauty and the practice of virtue. The soul, absorbed in the sensible world and identified with the body and its pleasures, is awakened from this absorption by the experience of beautiful forms — beautiful faces, beautiful music, beautiful deeds. These earthly beauties are not traps but traces or images of a higher beauty, and the properly philosophical response to them is not attachment but the recognition that they point beyond themselves. Through this recognition the soul begins the practice of virtue, which purifies it from the habits of body-identification and prepares it for higher contemplation. This stage is beautifully described in Ennead I.6, "On Beauty," which traces the ascent from the beauty of physical forms through the beauty of souls, customs, and sciences, to the Beauty itself — the Intellect.

The second stage is philosophical contemplation at the level of Intellect. Through sustained mathematical and philosophical reflection, the soul ascends from engagement with beautiful particulars to contemplation of the universal Forms in the Intellect — the eternal patterns of all that is, the ideas of Beauty, Justice, Wisdom, and Being itself. At this level, the soul participates in the life of the Intellect and begins to recognise its own deepest nature as akin to and continuous with the divine Intellect. This is not merely abstract intellectual exercise: for Plotinus, genuine philosophical thinking is a spiritual practice, a form of purification and elevation through which the soul literally becomes what it contemplates. The philosopher who truly thinks the eternal Forms is, in the act of that thinking, living the life of the Intellect — already halfway home.

The third stage — mystical union with the One — surpasses even Intellect and admits of no adequate description, because all description implies a duality between describer and described, and what is sought here is the annihilation of all duality. The soul must "simplify" itself beyond discursive thought and even beyond the unitary self-knowing of Intellect, stripping away every conceptual determination until nothing remains but pure, undifferentiated, will-less awareness — and in that flash of simplicity, the soul finds itself united with the One, knowing it by becoming it. Plotinus describes this experience in Ennead VI.9 with celebrated economy: he phuge monou pros monon — "the flight of the alone to the Alone." The paradox is complete: the most intimate and most certain of all experiences is the one least susceptible to verbal expression. Plotinus insists, however, that in this union the soul does not cease to exist or lose itself — it rather finds its truest nature, recognising that what it has always most deeply been is a ray of that divine light from which it seemingly departed. The role of beauty, eros, and philosophical dialectic as means of ascent, and of theurgy as a lower preparatory discipline (a path Plotinus himself did not favour, preferring pure contemplation), would be developed and systematised by his student Porphyry and above all by Iamblichus.

The Enneads

The Enneads — the collected philosophical writings of Plotinus — owe their form entirely to the editorial genius of Porphyry, who gathered and organised his teacher's writings after Plotinus's death. Plotinus had composed his treatises relatively late in life, beginning only when he was around fifty, and he took no pains to revise or publish them — his vision was so poor that he could not reread what he had written. Porphyry received the manuscripts, corrected them, and arranged the 54 individual treatises into six groups of nine (hence Enneads, from the Greek ennea, nine), organising them thematically from the least to the most elevated metaphysical topics: the first Ennead treating ethical and psychological questions; the second and third treating cosmology and the natural world; the fourth, Soul; the fifth, Intellect; and the sixth, the One and the relation of being to the One.

The Enneads are remarkable literary-philosophical documents, unlike anything else in ancient philosophy. They are not systematic treatises in the manner of Aristotle's Metaphysics or Aquinas's Summa Theologica but records of philosophical seminar discussions — exploratory, circling, often beginning in medias res with a philosophical puzzle and working through it in a characteristically spiralling style that revisits the same questions from different angles with increasing depth. Key texts include: Ennead I.6 ("On Beauty"), the accessible gateway text that opens the path of ascent through the experience of beautiful forms; Ennead V.1 ("On the Three Primary Hypostases"), the clearest single account of Plotinus's metaphysical architecture; and Ennead VI.9 ("On the Good, or the One"), the culminating mystical text that describes, as far as language allows, the experience of union with the One. Each of these texts rewards repeated reading at different stages of philosophical and spiritual development.

Though Plotinus considered himself simply a commentator on Plato — clarifying and systematising what Plato had already taught — his synthesis was profoundly original in a way that he himself might not have fully recognised. The three-hypostasis system, the detailed account of emanation and return, the theory of matter as privation, the doctrine of the undescended intellect — none of these is straightforwardly present in Plato's texts, however much they may be argued to follow from them. The first complete Latin translation of the Enneads was made by Marsilio Ficino in Florence and published in 1492 — a date of extraordinary cultural significance, in the same year as Columbus's voyage and Ficino's own death. This translation made the full Plotinian system available to the Renaissance world and, through the Renaissance, to the modern world. The difficulty and richness of Plotinus's Greek prose — which combines the highest technical rigour with passages of visionary intensity and unexpected metaphorical beauty — has continued to reward the efforts of translators and commentators from A.H. Armstrong to Pierre Hadot and Lloyd Gerson in the modern period.

Legacy & Influence

The immediate circle of Plotinus's students produced thinkers whose influence extended far beyond philosophy. Porphyry, the most literary of his disciples, edited the Enneads, wrote the celebrated Isagoge (Introduction to Aristotle's Categories) that became a standard textbook throughout the Middle Ages, composed a biography of Pythagoras, and wrote works on vegetarianism, astrology, and the critique of Christianity. Amelius continued in the philosophical tradition, while Iamblichus — the most transformative of Plotinus's intellectual heirs — radically modified Neoplatonism in a theurgical direction, insisting that ritual practice was indispensable for the soul's salvation and adding vast numbers of ontological levels to the Plotinian hierarchy. The tension between Plotinus's contemplative, intellectualist approach and Iamblichus's theurgical emphasis would run through all subsequent Neoplatonism.

The impact of Plotinian thought on early Christianity was decisive and multifaceted. Origen, who had also studied with Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria, developed a Christian theology that shares many structural features with Neoplatonism. The Cappadocian Fathers — Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus — absorbed Plotinian ideas into their Christian theology, particularly Gregory of Nyssa's mystical theology of the soul's unending ascent toward the infinite God. Most consequentially, Augustine of Hippo's decisive encounter with Neoplatonism — through Marius Victorinus's Latin translations of Plotinus — shaped the entire subsequent development of Western Christian theology. In his Confessions, Augustine describes how reading the Neoplatonists taught him to conceive of God and the soul as non-material realities, dissolving his earlier materialism and opening the way for his conversion; and the Plotinian structure of the soul's restless movement toward its divine source — "our heart is restless until it finds rest in Thee" — pervades the Confessions from beginning to end. Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500 CE) created an explicitly Neoplatonic Christian mystical theology that shaped the entire medieval tradition of mysticism, from John Scottus Eriugena through Meister Eckhart to the Rhineland mystics.

The transmission of Neoplatonism to the Islamic world occurred primarily through the Theology of Aristotle — a text falsely attributed to Aristotle but actually consisting of extracts from the Enneads — and through Proclus's Elements of Theology. Through these texts, Plotinian ideas of emanation, the One, and the soul's return deeply influenced al-Farabi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), and Ibn Rushd (Averroës), and through them the entire tradition of Islamic philosophy and mysticism, including Sufism. The rediscovery of the Enneads in the Renaissance through Ficino's 1492 translation gave them a new lease of life in Western culture, directly influencing Giordano Bruno, John Dee, the Cambridge Platonists (Henry More, Ralph Cudworth), and the Romantic philosophers (Schelling, Coleridge). The 20th century saw a major revival of Plotinian scholarship through scholars including A.H. Armstrong, whose magisterial translation and edition of the Enneads (1966–88) remains the standard, Pierre Hadot, who emphasised the spiritual-exercise character of ancient philosophy, and Lloyd Gerson, whose analytic engagement with Plotinus has opened his thought to a new generation of philosophers.
