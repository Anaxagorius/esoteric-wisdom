Enoch & Metatron

Enoch — the seventh patriarch from Adam, who 'walked with God and was not, for God took him' — occupies a unique place in the Abrahamic mystical traditions as the human being most fully translated into the divine realm. In Jewish mystical literature he is identified with Metatron, the supreme angel, the 'lesser YHWH', and the celestial scribe who records the deeds of all living beings. Together, Enoch and Metatron form one of the most remarkable figures in the entire history of religious thought.

The Biblical Enoch

The biblical account of Enoch is extraordinarily brief — a mere three verses in Genesis 5:21–24 — yet its implications have been extraordinarily far-reaching. Unlike all the other patriarchs listed in the genealogy, whose entries end with the formula "and he died," Enoch's entry concludes: "Enoch walked faithfully with God; then he was no more, because God took him." This cryptic statement — the Hebrew lāqaḥ ("took") — implies that Enoch did not die but was translated bodily into the divine presence, a fate shared in the Hebrew Bible only with the prophet Elijah, who ascended to heaven in a chariot of fire.

Enoch's position as the seventh from Adam carries significant symbolism. In Mesopotamian tradition, the seventh antediluvian king was Enmeduranki of Sippar, a figure associated with divine wisdom, astronomical knowledge, and the arts of divination — suggesting that the biblical Enoch draws on or reflects an ancient Near Eastern tradition of a human sage who received divine revelation before the Flood. Enoch's lifespan of 365 years — precisely the number of days in a solar year — further reinforces his connection with calendrical and astronomical knowledge: in the Enochic literature that developed around this figure, one of his primary roles is as the recipient of divine instruction regarding the movements of the sun, moon, and stars.

The rabbis of the Talmudic period were initially somewhat uneasy with the Enoch tradition, perhaps because the extensive and apocalyptically charged Enochic literature had been excluded from the Hebrew biblical canon. Some rabbinical sources suggest that Enoch was not actually righteous but was taken before he could sin; others maintain that he was indeed a supreme saint. The dominant tradition, however, recognised his unique status as one who had passed through death's door without dying and emerged on the other side as something more than human.

The Books of Enoch

The brief biblical account of Enoch generated one of the most extensive bodies of pseudepigraphal literature in the ancient world. The three major Enochic texts represent very different periods and theological traditions, yet all share the fundamental narrative of Enoch's heavenly journeys and his reception of divine knowledge.

1 Enoch (the Ethiopic Enoch) is a composite work, probably compiled between the 3rd century BCE and the 1st century CE, preserved complete only in the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) language and now consisting of five major sections: the Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36), the Book of the Similitudes or Parables (chapters 37–71), the Astronomical Book (chapters 72–82), the Book of Dream Visions (chapters 83–90), and the Epistle of Enoch (chapters 91–108). The Book of the Watchers contains the famous narrative of the Watchers (Grigori) — two hundred angels who descended to earth, mated with human women, and taught forbidden arts (weapons-making, sorcery, cosmetics, astrology), producing the hybrid giant Nephilim. This narrative, an elaboration of the brief account in Genesis 6:1–4, became one of the most influential mythological frameworks in subsequent Jewish, Christian, and Islamic literature.

2 Enoch (the Slavonic Enoch or Book of the Secrets of Enoch) is preserved only in Old Church Slavonic and probably dates from the 1st century CE. It contains Enoch's ascent through ten heavens, his reception of divine knowledge from God himself, and the dictation of 360 books of secret wisdom to his son Methuselah. 3 Enoch (the Sefer Hekhalot, or Book of the Heavenly Palaces) is a Hebrew text probably compiled in the 5th–6th centuries CE and attributed to Rabbi Ishmael ben Elisha, who ascends to heaven and meets the supreme angel Metatron — whom Metatron reveals to be none other than the patriarch Enoch, transformed.

The Heavenly Ascent

The Merkabah ("divine chariot") tradition of Jewish mysticism — named after the chariot-throne vision of Ezekiel 1 — represents one of the most dramatic and technically elaborate mystical traditions in the history of religion. Beginning perhaps in the 1st century CE and flourishing throughout the Talmudic and post-Talmudic periods (2nd–8th centuries CE), Merkabah mysticism involved the visionary ascent of the practitioner through the seven heavens and the seven heavenly palaces (hekhalot) to the throne-chariot (Merkabah) of God, where the mystic hoped to behold the divine Glory (Kavod) and receive heavenly secrets.

Enoch's ascent as described in the Enochic literature prefigures this tradition. In his ascents, Enoch passes through successive heavenly realms, encountering angels, cosmic machinery (the storehouses of wind, snow, and dew), and the divine throne itself. He receives cosmic knowledge — the calendar, the movements of the heavenly bodies, the identity of the fallen Watchers, and the secrets of the final judgement — that he is instructed to transmit to the righteous on earth.

The transformation of Enoch during his heavenly ascent is described in breathtaking terms in 3 Enoch: his flesh becomes flames, his sinews blazing fire, his bones glowing coals, his eyeballs like torches of fire, the hairs of his head like flames, all his limbs like wings. God enlarges him so that his height equals the height of the world, and gives him a throne beside the divine throne, a garment of glory, a crown with 49 jewels, and a new name. This transformation — the deification of the human being through intimate divine contact — represents one of the most radical statements of human-divine unity in the entire Jewish mystical tradition.

Metatron in Jewish Mysticism

In 3 Enoch, the angel Metatron identifies himself to Rabbi Ishmael as the transformed Enoch, and his role and titles reveal him as the supreme being of the angelic realm, second only to God. He is called the "Prince of the Divine Presence" (Sar ha-Panim), the "lesser YHWH" (3 Enoch 12 — "He called me by the name 'the lesser YHWH' before all his heavenly household"), and is seated upon a throne beside the divine throne, commanding the entire heavenly host.

Metatron's name is one of the great puzzles of Jewish mystical scholarship. Proposed etymologies include: the Latin metator (a soldier who prepares the way); the Greek meta-thronos (one who stands behind the throne); the Greek metathronios (one who shares the throne); or an Aramaic derivation related to divine authority. None of these etymologies has achieved scholarly consensus, and the name may ultimately be an artificial construction designed to contain the numerical value 314 — equal to the divine name Shaddai (Almighty) in Hebrew gematria.

As the heavenly scribe, Metatron records the deeds of all humanity in the heavenly books, and his 72 names (corresponding to the 72 names of God) give him authority over every aspect of the created order. His role as the "Prince of the Torah" links him directly to the transmission of divine wisdom to human beings. In later Kabbalistic tradition, Metatron is associated with the highest Sephirah accessible to angelic beings (Keter or Binah, depending on the tradition) and is sometimes identified with the Shekhinah, the divine presence that accompanied Israel in exile.

Legacy in Kabbalah & Esotericism

The Enoch-Metatron tradition exercised a profound and enduring influence on the subsequent development of Jewish mysticism and Western esotericism. In the Zohar, the central text of Kabbalistic mysticism composed in 13th-century Spain by Moses de León (though attributed to Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai), Metatron appears as the regent of the world of Yetzirah (the formative world), the manager of the divine economy in the lower worlds. He is identified with the biblical angel who accompanied Israel through the wilderness — "My name is in him" (Exodus 23:21) — and with the Primordial Human (Adam Kadmon) in certain interpretive strands.

The concept of "Metatron's Cube" — a sacred geometric figure derived from the Fruit of Life (thirteen circles in the Flower of Life pattern) by connecting the centre of each circle to every other centre — has become one of the most widely recognised symbols in contemporary sacred geometry and New Age spirituality. Each of the five Platonic solids can be derived from this figure, making it a kind of geometric summa of the fundamental forms underlying physical reality. While the specific term "Metatron's Cube" appears to be a relatively recent development, its roots lie in the Kabbalistic association of Metatron with the divine creative geometry of the Sefirot.

In the modern esoteric revival, Metatron has become a prominent figure in New Age channelling traditions, angel communication, and energy healing modalities such as Reiki and Merkabah meditation. He is typically invoked as a guide for those working with light, sacred geometry, and the highest frequencies of divine consciousness. The Enochic tradition more broadly continues to influence ceremonial magic (through John Dee's Enochian system of angelic communication, developed in the 1580s), Thelema, and the broader Western magical tradition.
