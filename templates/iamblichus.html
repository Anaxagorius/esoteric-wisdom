Iamblichus

Iamblichus of Chalcis — the Syrian philosopher who studied under Porphyry and founded his own flourishing school at Apamea — represents one of the most decisive turning points in the history of Neoplatonism. Where his teacher Porphyry had maintained that philosophical contemplation alone could elevate the soul to union with the divine, Iamblichus insisted that ritual theurgy — the 'working of the gods' — was indispensable for human salvation, because the human soul was too deeply mired in matter to ascend by intellect alone.

Life & Background

Iamblichus was born c. 245 CE at Chalcis in Coele-Syria, a region of what is now modern Syria, and came from an aristocratic family that also produced the earlier Syrian novelist Iamblichus, author of the Babyloniaca. The philosophical Iamblichus received his initial training under the Peripatetic philosopher Anatolius, who gave him a thorough grounding in Aristotelian logic and philosophy, before making the journey westward — most probably to Rome or Sicily — to study under Porphyry, who had been the most intimate student and literary executor of Plotinus. How long Iamblichus remained under Porphyry's tutelage is unknown, but the intellectual relationship was clearly formative: Iamblichus read Porphyry closely enough to subject his positions to systematic and detailed critique, and the great controversy over theurgy versus contemplation that defines much of Iamblichus's surviving work can only be understood against the background of this personal philosophical relationship.

After his studies in the West, Iamblichus returned to Syria and established his own school at Apamea on the Orontes, which became one of the most flourishing and influential philosophical institutions of late antiquity. The school attracted students from across the eastern Roman Empire, many of whom went on to occupy important positions as teachers, administrators, and advisers at the highest levels of society. Iamblichus himself was held in extraordinary reverence by later Neoplatonists — the Emperor Julian the Apostate (r. 361–363 CE), who made a concerted effort to revive paganism as the official religion of the Roman Empire, regarded Iamblichus as second only to Plato and Pythagoras among all thinkers and addressed him in a letter as "a god among men." This imperial veneration is a measure of Iamblichus's cultural and religious authority in his own time, extending far beyond the usual boundaries of academic philosophy.

Like Pythagoras before him, Iamblichus attracted miraculous stories. Students reportedly witnessed him levitating ten cubits into the air whilst at prayer, his body shining with golden and silver light. Such accounts contrast markedly with the more austere, intellectual image of Plotinus — who, according to Porphyry, never spoke of his own mystical experiences unless directly questioned — and reflect the very different atmosphere of Iamblichus's school, in which theurgical practice and divine manifestation were regarded as integral to the philosophical life. Iamblichus was also a prolific writer, with over twenty-five works known, of which only a handful survive in substantial form: the large collection known as On the Pythagorean Life (with its companion volumes on Pythagorean mathematics, arithmetic, geometry, and music), De Mysteriis (On the Mysteries), and fragments of his commentaries on Plato and Aristotle preserved in later sources. Much of his philosophical output survives only through the extensive quotations and summaries in later Neoplatonic authors, particularly Proclus.

Theurgy vs. Contemplation

The fundamental theological and philosophical dispute between Porphyry and Iamblichus over the means of the soul's ascent to the divine is the central question animating Iamblichus's major surviving work De Mysteriis (On the Mysteries of Egypt). Porphyry had sent a letter — the Letter to Anebo, addressed to an Egyptian priest — raising critical and sceptical questions about the basis of theurgical practices: sacrifice, the invocation of daimones, the use of oracles and divination. Underlying these specific questions was a philosophical position: Porphyry maintained, broadly following Plotinus, that the soul's highest faculty — intellect — was continuous with and akin to the divine Intellect, and that through philosophical contemplation alone the soul could achieve purification and union with the Good. On this view, ritual practices were at best a pedagogical aid for those not yet capable of pure intellectual contemplation, not an ontologically necessary means of divine contact.

Iamblichus's response — written in the persona of the Egyptian priest "Abammon" — constitutes a systematic and comprehensive counter-argument. His core thesis is a radical reassessment of the human soul's metaphysical position. In Plotinus's system, the soul retains a portion of itself — an "undescended soul" — permanently in contact with the divine Intellect, which means that philosophical contemplation can in principle reconnect the lower, embodied soul with its divine source. Iamblichus denied this: in his view, the human soul has fully and irrevocably descended into matter, and there is no part of it that remains untouched by the conditions of embodied existence. The consequence is decisive — no amount of human intellectual effort, however sustained and however pure, can bridge the infinite qualitative gulf between the finite human soul and the transcendent divine. Only the gods themselves can accomplish this bridging, and they do so through the gift of theurgy: a system of sacred rites, symbols, and practices through which the divine condescends to act in and through the material world, elevating the soul from below rather than requiring it to ascend under its own power.

The theological implications of this position are profound and reach far beyond the Neoplatonic controversy. Iamblichus is, in effect, arguing for a doctrine analogous to grace — the soul requires divine initiative, not merely human effort. Philosophy and virtue are necessary preparations but not sufficient causes of salvation; what is required is theurgical practice, through which the soul opens itself to and participates in the divine activity descending from above. This represents a fundamental shift in the relationship between the human and the divine: from the Plotinian model of a self-sufficient contemplative ascent to the Iamblichan model of a worshipful, receptive, and ritually structured opening to divine grace. The parallels with developments in Christian theology in the same period — particularly the Augustinian doctrine of grace against Pelagian self-sufficiency — are striking and historically significant, reflecting a broader late antique shift in religious sensibility away from the confidence in human reason and toward dependence on divine gift.

On the Mysteries

De Mysteriis Aegyptiorum — "On the Egyptian Mysteries" — is Iamblichus's masterwork and one of the most remarkable philosophical texts of late antiquity. Written in the persona of the Egyptian priest "Abammon" responding point by point to Porphyry's critical Letter to Anebo, it is simultaneously a systematic defence of traditional religious practice, a comprehensive philosophical theology of the divine hierarchy, and a detailed account of how theurgical operations function. The work is structured across ten books, moving from the fundamental metaphysical distinctions between gods, daimones, heroes, and souls, through the theory of divine inspiration and oracular prophecy, to the principles underlying sacrifice and theurgy, and culminating in a vision of the soul's ultimate divinisation through theurgical practice. It is the most comprehensive surviving account of what Iamblichus understood by theurgical Neoplatonism and remains the primary source for understanding the religious dimension of late Neoplatonism as a whole.

Central to the theory of theurgical action in De Mysteriis is the concept of synthemata and symbola — sacred signs, objects, words, gestures, herbs, stones, and sounds that have been placed in the material world by the gods themselves as traces and vehicles of their divine power. These divine symbols are not human conventions or arbitrary signs but genuine ontological connections between the material and the divine, established not by human art or philosophy but by divine providence. The crucial consequence is that the efficacy of theurgical ritual does not depend on the mental state, the moral purity, or even the conscious intention of the practitioner — it depends on the objective power of the divine symbols themselves and on the divine will to act through them. This is the most radical aspect of Iamblichus's theory and the one most sharply in contrast with the Plotinian emphasis on the soul's own intellectual and moral effort. The gods act; the theurgist creates the conditions in which that divine action can take place.

The hierarchy of ritual types described in De Mysteriis corresponds to the different levels of the divine hierarchy. At the highest level, the approach to the supreme gods is made through hymns, prayers, and the contemplative elevation of the soul; material sacrifice, with its emphasis on blood and material offerings, is appropriate for the lower material daimones whose nature is more closely bound to matter; and theurgy proper — working directly with divine fire and divine light — is reserved for the highest theurgical operations aimed at the highest gods. The culminating experience that all genuine theurgy aims at is what Iamblichus calls autopsia — the direct, unmediated vision of the divine light, in which the practitioner sees, with both intellectual and quasi-sensory clarity, the divine fire that is the ultimate nature of reality. This is not quite the same as Plotinus's ineffable union with the One, for it retains more of the character of a vision — a beholding — rather than a total dissolution of the boundary between self and divine. But it is clearly the highest spiritual experience that Iamblichan theurgy aims to achieve, and the goal toward which the entire elaborate edifice of divine symbols and ritual actions is directed.

The Divine Hierarchy

One of the most characteristic and influential features of Iamblichus's philosophical system is the vast expansion of the metaphysical hierarchy beyond the three hypostases of Plotinus. Where Plotinus had posited three fundamental levels — the One, the Intellect, and the Soul — Iamblichus multiplied the ontological levels to a degree that later commentators found both systematically necessary and somewhat bewildering. At the summit, Iamblichus posited an Ineffable First Principle entirely beyond even the One of Plotinus — an absolute that transcends any positive description whatsoever. Below this stands the One of Plotinus, itself now a distinct level. From the One proceed the Henads — divine unities corresponding to the traditional Olympian gods — each of which is the source of a particular divine quality or power that pervades the entire hierarchy below it. Below the Henads come subdivided levels of Intellect and Soul, each with their own internal differentiations, and finally the material world with its elemental and material principles.

The rationale for this multiplication of levels is philosophical as well as religious. Iamblichus employs a principle sometimes called the "law of mean terms" or the continuity principle: between any two radically different ontological levels there must be intermediate levels that share characteristics of both, mediating between them and ensuring a smooth, unbroken continuum from the highest to the lowest. This principle is not merely an arbitrary proliferation of entities but reflects a genuine metaphysical insight: radical ontological differences cannot be bridged by a single step, and a hierarchical system that jumps directly from the infinite to the finite, or from pure unity to complex multiplicity, fails to account for the richness and gradation of reality as we encounter it. Each additional level in Iamblichus's hierarchy serves to make intelligible a transition that would otherwise appear abrupt and inexplicable.

Particularly important in Iamblichus's metaphysics is his departure from Plotinus on the status of the individual human soul. In Plotinus's system, the soul retains an "undescended" portion at the level of the divine Intellect, which means it has never fully left its divine home and can return to it by intellectual contemplation alone. Iamblichus firmly denied this: the human soul, in his view, has fully and irreversibly descended into embodiment and lost its connection with the divine levels. This is not a counsel of despair but rather the philosophical foundation of the necessity of theurgy: precisely because the soul cannot re-ascend under its own power, it requires the divinely instituted means of the Chaldean Oracles and the theurgical rites associated with them. The Chaldean Oracles — a collection of 2nd-century CE hexameter verses attributed to Julian the Theurgist and presenting a revealed theology of the divine hierarchy, soul's descent, and salvation — Iamblichus regarded as a text of supreme authority, equivalent to the sacred writings of Egypt and Chaldea, and devoted an extensive commentary (now lost) to their interpretation. The Oracles became, through Iamblichus's influence, the canonical scripture of later Neoplatonism.

Legacy & Influence

The influence of Iamblichus on all subsequent Neoplatonism was decisive and comprehensive. Proclus of Athens (412–485 CE), universally regarded as the greatest systematic thinker of late Neoplatonism and the author of the Elements of Theology and the Platonic Theology, followed Iamblichus's theurgical approach and expanded the hierarchical metaphysics still further, producing a system of extraordinary complexity and rigour in which every level of reality was correlated with the others through precise mathematical and ontological relationships. Through Proclus, Iamblichan Neoplatonism entered the Christian theological tradition via Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, whose celestial and ecclesiastical hierarchies reflect an unmistakably Proclean — and hence ultimately Iamblichan — metaphysical architecture. The Emperor Julian the Apostate's brief but intense attempt to revive paganism as an Iamblichan theurgical religion demonstrates the political as well as philosophical significance of Iamblichus's legacy: here was a philosophy that could serve as the intellectual and spiritual foundation of an entire civilisation's religious life.

The transmission of Iamblichan Neoplatonism to the Renaissance occurred primarily through Proclus, whose works were translated into Latin by George of Trebizond, Ficino, and others in the 15th and 16th centuries. Ficino's concept of natural magic — the drawing down of celestial influences through the use of materials, images, music, and ritual actions sympathetically connected to the desired planet — depends heavily on Iamblichan principles of theurgical sympathy, even when Ficino carefully sought to distinguish his own practice from what he called "demonic" magic. Cornelius Agrippa's systematic account of magical practice in De Occulta Philosophia (1531) draws on the same Iamblichan inheritance through Ficino. The principle that ritual actions possess objective efficacy independent of the practitioner's subjective state — one of the most distinctive and controversial features of Iamblichus's theory — found a natural home in ceremonial magic traditions that needed to account for why magical operations performed with correct procedure produce reliable results.

In the modern period, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and the subsequent ceremonial magic tradition took seriously Iamblichus's fundamental insight that ritual symbols have objective divine efficacy — that the practitioner who correctly assembles the appropriate divine symbols creates a real ontological connection with the corresponding divine power. The contemporary scholarly reassessment of Iamblichus has been equally significant. Gregory Shaw's Theurgy and the Soul (1995), John Finamore's studies of Iamblichus's psychology, and Emma Clarke's work on De Mysteriis have collectively argued that Iamblichus's theurgical philosophy deserves serious consideration not as a symptom of late antique religious decline — as earlier scholars tended to view it — but as a sophisticated, internally coherent response to the genuine philosophical problem of how finite embodied beings can relate to an infinite transcendent divine. The debate between Iamblichus and Porphyry over theurgy versus contemplation, grace versus self-effort, ritual versus reason, remains one of the most fundamental and unresolved questions in the philosophy of religion.
