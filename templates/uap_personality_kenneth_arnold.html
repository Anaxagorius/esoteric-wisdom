Kenneth Arnold

"The Man Who Named the Flying Saucer" · 1915–1984

Brief Biography

Kenneth Albert Arnold was born on 29 March 1915 in Sebeka, Minnesota. He worked as a successful fire equipment salesman covering the Pacific Northwest, and was a highly experienced private pilot — a Federal Aviation Administration-certified pilot with some 4,000 hours of flight time and a reputation for careful, meticulous observation. He owned and flew a CallAir A-2 and later a Piper Tri-Pacer. He was also a deputy sheriff in Boise, Idaho, and a candidate for political office. By all accounts, Arnold was a thoroughly grounded, credible witness — the kind the emerging Air Force investigation apparatus would take seriously if it chose to.

He spent the years following his 1947 sighting actively investigating UFOs himself, collaborated with Ray Palmer on a book, and remained an outspoken figure in early ufology until his death on 16 January 1984 in Bellevue, Washington. He consistently maintained the accuracy and importance of what he saw.

The Mt. Rainier Sighting — 24 June 1947

On the afternoon of 24 June 1947, Arnold was flying his private aircraft near Mount Rainier, Washington, en route from Chehalis to Yakima, at an altitude of approximately 9,200 feet. He had agreed to search for a Marine Corps C-46 transport aircraft that had gone down in the area. The weather was clear and visibility excellent.

At approximately 3:00 pm he noticed a bright flash of light to his north. Assuming it might be military aircraft, he scanned the sky and observed a chain of nine objects flying in a diagonal echelon formation from north to south. They appeared to be flat and roughly crescent- or heel-shaped — he specifically noted they were not circular — and they were weaving among the mountain peaks. Arnold timed them between Mt. Rainier and Mt. Adams (approximately 50 miles) and calculated their speed at a then-extraordinary 1,700 mph — more than twice the speed of any known aircraft in 1947.

Crucially, Arnold described their motion as that of "a saucer if you skip it across water" — meaning they moved with a skipping, undulating motion. The journalist who covered the story, Bill Bequette of the East Oregonian, misinterpreted this to mean the objects were saucer-shaped, and the phrase "flying saucer" entered the language. Arnold always insisted the objects were more crescent- or boomerang-shaped, not disc-shaped.

Aftermath and Investigation

Arnold's account was reported by wire services and became a national story within days. He was interviewed by the Army Air Forces' A-2 intelligence officer at Pendleton, Oregon, and by representatives of what would become Project Sign. His account was deemed credible — Arnold's aviation experience and his specific, technical description of speeds, altitudes, and formation flying distinguished his report from those of purely amateur observers.

Arnold's sighting triggered a wave of thousands of copycat reports across the United States in the summer of 1947, directly contributing to the establishment of the first official USAF investigation — Project Sign (1948). It also set the media and cultural framework through which all subsequent UFO reports would be interpreted. Arnold himself became a sought-after public speaker and eventually co-authored The Coming of the Saucers (1952) with Ray Palmer, editor of Amazing Stories.

The Air Force's explanation — that Arnold had seen a formation of snow geese or a mirage — was rejected by Arnold and by many subsequent analysts. The objects' estimated speed at nearly twice the sound barrier (at a time when no aircraft had yet broken it — that came with Chuck Yeager's X-1 flight in October 1947) and their highly coherent, controlled formation flight remains unaccounted for by any mundane explanation.

Historical Significance

Kenneth Arnold's sighting is the foundational event of the modern UFO era. He did not invent the phenomenon — unexplained aerial objects had been reported throughout history and in significant clusters during WWII (the "Foo Fighters") — but his sighting, due to his credibility, the quality of his report, and the accidental coinage of "flying saucer," crystallised the phenomenon into a cultural object. Every element of subsequent UFO discourse — the credible witness, the formation flight, the impossible speed, the government investigation, the inadequate official explanation — appeared first in the Arnold case.

Researchers including Richard Dolan, Jacques Vallée, and Leslie Kean have noted that Arnold's case has never been satisfactorily explained, and that the specific aerodynamic characteristics he described — flat, crescent-shaped objects flying in tight formation at extreme speed — bear little resemblance to any natural phenomenon or misidentification target that has ever been proposed.

"What I saw that day over the Cascades was as real as the mountains themselves. I am a pilot. I know what aircraft look like. These were not any aircraft I or anyone else had ever seen."
— Kenneth Arnold
