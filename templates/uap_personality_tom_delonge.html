Tom DeLonge

"The Rock Star Who Opened the File" · b. 1975

Brief Biography

Thomas Matthew DeLonge was born on 13 December 1975 in Poway, California. He co-founded the punk-pop band Blink-182 in 1992, which became one of the best-selling bands of the late 1990s and 2000s, selling over 50 million records. Alongside his music career, DeLonge maintained a consuming private interest in UAP that he has described as dating to his adolescence — an obsession so intense that it eventually contributed to tensions within the band and his departure in 2015.

In 2017, DeLonge founded To The Stars Academy of Arts and Science (TTSA), assembling a team of former senior intelligence, DoD, and aerospace officials — including Luis Elizondo, Hal Puthoff, Jim Semivan, and Christopher Mellon — that represented the most remarkable concentration of insider talent ever assembled for a civilian UAP enterprise. TTSA's subsequent New York Times partnership catalysed the most significant UAP disclosure event since Project Blue Book's closure.

To The Stars Academy and the NYT Bombshell

The October 2017 launch of TTSA was followed in December 2017 by a front-page New York Times story — co-bylined by Leslie Kean, Ralph Blumenthal, and Helene Cooper — revealing the existence of the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), releasing the first two officially confirmed military UAP videos, and introducing Luis Elizondo to the public as a former senior intelligence official who had resigned over the government's inadequate response to UAP. The article was directly connected to DeLonge's TTSA operation.

The strategic logic was DeLonge's: rather than pursuing leaks or FOIA requests, he would leverage his celebrity access to cultivate relationships with government insiders, then create a public-private vehicle that could receive, process, and appropriately release sensitive information. The WikiLeaks-released Podesta emails confirmed that DeLonge had extensive communications with John Podesta (Clinton's campaign chairman and former Obama White House chief of staff) and aerospace executive Ron Pandolfi regarding UAP information access.

Skepticism and Complexity

DeLonge is a genuinely complex figure in UAP history. His non-institutional background — rock musician rather than scientist, journalist, or government official — made him both an unusual messenger and, for some, an easily dismissible one. His public statements about the phenomenon have sometimes been imprecise or speculative in ways that attracted mockery. At the same time, the team he assembled and the results he achieved — the New York Times article, the Pentagon's subsequent acknowledgement of the videos, the launch of the congressional UAP investigation process — represent genuine, consequential achievements that no more credentialled figure had managed in decades of trying. Whatever DeLonge's limitations as a theorist, his role as a catalyst for institutional change in the UAP landscape is historically significant.
