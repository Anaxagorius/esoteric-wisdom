Runes & Runestones

Carved in stone, whispered by Odin, wielded by Vikings and skalds — runes are among humanity's most potent symbols. Ancient alphabet, living oracle, and sacred key to the cosmic order.

✦ What Are Runes?

Runes are the letters of an ancient Germanic writing system used by Norse, Anglo-Saxon, and other Germanic peoples from roughly the 2nd century CE onward. But runes were never merely an alphabet. The word rune derives from the Proto-Germanic *rūnō — meaning "secret," "mystery," or "whisper." To carve a rune was to inscribe a living force into the material world.

Each runic character is not simply a phonetic symbol but a concept — a force of nature, a cosmic principle, a living energy. The rune ᚠ (Fehu) does not just sound like "F"; it embodies wealth, cattle, primal fire, and the flowing vitality of the universe. In this sense, runes are closer to Chinese ideograms or Egyptian hieroglyphs than to a modern alphabet.

A runestone is a raised stone bearing runic inscriptions. Thousands survive across Scandinavia — particularly in Sweden, where over 2,500 have been found — raised as memorials, boundary markers, or declarations of power. The most famous are the Jelling stones of Denmark (c. 960–985 CE), raised by King Harald Bluetooth to commemorate the conversion of Denmark to Christianity, standing at the very threshold between old and new worlds.

In esoteric practice, runes function as a divinatory and magical system with extraordinary depth. Each rune governs a sphere of existence — love, conflict, fertility, death, fate, transformation — and consulting them is less about predicting a fixed future than about illuminating the hidden currents already shaping your path.

Rune magic (galdr) involved chanting the rune's name, carving it into objects, and meditating on its essence to align with its power. Warriors carved runes onto swords and shields. Healers inscribed them on amulets. Poets wove them into verse to invoke protection, love, or victory.

Today, runes are used for meditation, self-reflection, divination, and as spiritual anchors — a way of conversing with the deep, archaic wisdom encoded in Northern European cosmology and the mythology of the Norse gods.

📜 Odin and the Discovery of Runes

According to the Norse poem Hávamál ("Sayings of the High One"), the god Odin hung himself upside-down from the great world-tree Yggdrasil for nine days and nine nights, wounded by his own spear, fasting and in agony — a voluntary sacrifice of self to Self, in order to discover the runes. "I took up the runes, screaming I took them, then I fell back from there." The act is one of the oldest recorded accounts of shamanic initiation: entering the void to seize cosmic knowledge. This myth places runes not as human invention but as forces discovered within the fabric of existence.

✦ A History Carved in Stone

The earliest runic inscriptions date to around 150–200 CE, found on objects such as combs, buckles, and spearheads across what is now Denmark and northern Germany. The origin of the runic alphabet is debated — scholars trace strong parallels to the Old Italic alphabets (especially Etruscan or Latin), suggesting Germanic peoples encountered these scripts through contact with Roman or Mediterranean cultures and adapted them into a system uniquely suited to carving in wood, bone, and stone.

The earliest full runic alphabet, the Elder Futhark, consists of 24 runes arranged in three groups of eight called ættir (families). It was used across the Germanic world from roughly 150–800 CE. The name "Futhark" derives from the first six runes: ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ.

During the Viking Age (c. 793–1066 CE), the Younger Futhark emerged in Scandinavia — a reduced 16-rune alphabet adapted to phonetic shifts in the Old Norse language. Paradoxically, as Viking culture flourished and literacy spread, the alphabet shrank. Scholars suggest the condensed form retained a sacred quality: each character now carried even more concentrated meaning.

The Anglo-Saxons carried runes to Britain, expanding the Elder Futhark into the Futhorc — up to 33 characters — to account for additional Old English sounds. The Ruthwell Cross (c. 7th–8th century CE), carved in runic Futhorc with a passage from The Dream of the Rood, stands as one of the finest early Christian and runic monuments.

With the Christianisation of Scandinavia and Northern Europe, runic use gradually declined through the 11th–13th centuries — though in some Swedish regions, dalecarlian runes survived as a local script into the early 20th century, one of the longest continuous runic traditions in history.

The modern revival of runes as a divinatory and esoteric system gained momentum through the 19th-century Romantic interest in Norse mythology and accelerated in the 20th century through figures such as Ralph Blum, who popularised the casting of rune tiles in his 1982 book The Book of Runes. Today, runes sit at the heart of modern Norse paganism (Ásatrú), runic yoga, and a wide spectrum of spiritual practice worldwide.

Elder Futhark

24 runes · c. 150–800 CE · Used across all Germanic peoples. The oldest and most widely used runic alphabet — the foundation of all modern runic study and divination.

Younger Futhark

16 runes · c. 800–1100 CE · Evolved in Viking Age Scandinavia. Reduced but intensified — each rune shouldered greater linguistic and symbolic weight.

Anglo-Saxon Futhorc

28–33 runes · c. 400–1100 CE · Used in England and Frisia. Extended the Elder Futhark to capture Old English sounds. Found in manuscripts and stone monuments.

✧ The Elder Futhark — All 24 Runes ✧

Arranged in three ættir (families) of eight runes each.

Freyr's Ætt — Runes 1–8

ᚠ

Fehu

F · Cattle · Wealth

The first rune — primal fire, mobile wealth, and the power of creation. Fehu governs abundance, prosperity, and the flow of vital energy. It represents earned success and the wise stewardship of resources.

Keywords: Wealth, fertility, luck, success, new beginnings, fire
ᚢ

Uruz

U · Aurochs · Strength

Named for the auroch — the wild European ox, now extinct — Uruz embodies untamed physical strength, raw vitality, and endurance. It is the rune of the body's primal power and the force that drives transformation.

Keywords: Strength, health, primal force, courage, transition, wildness
ᚦ

Thurisaz

Th · Giant · Thorn

The thorn, the giant, the force of Thor's hammer. Thurisaz is protective and reactive — a weapon and a boundary. It guards against danger but warns of conflict or obstacles that must be confronted rather than avoided.

Keywords: Protection, conflict, Thor, directed force, boundaries, defence
ᚨ

Ansuz

A · Odin · The Divine

The rune of Odin, the All-Father, and of divine breath, language, and wisdom. Ansuz governs communication, inspiration, and messages from the higher mind. It is the rune of the poet and the prophet.

Keywords: Communication, wisdom, Odin, inspiration, messages, truth
ᚱ

Raidho

R · Wagon · Journey

The wheel, the road, the cosmic rhythm. Raidho governs travel — both physical journeys and the soul's journey through life. It speaks of right action, cycles, and the importance of direction and timing.

Keywords: Journey, movement, rhythm, right action, travel, cosmic order
ᚲ

Kenaz

K · Torch · Knowledge

The torch held in the dark. Kenaz is the rune of fire, craft, and illuminated knowledge. It represents creativity, artistic skill, inner light, and the spark that ignites transformation or passion.

Keywords: Knowledge, fire, creativity, craft, illumination, passion
ᚷ

Gebo

G · Gift · Partnership

The sacred gift and the reciprocal bond it creates. Gebo is the rune of generosity, partnership, and the balanced exchange that binds individuals, communities, and humans to the divine. It is one of very few runes with no reversed meaning.

Keywords: Gift, exchange, partnership, generosity, love, union
ᚹ

Wunjo

W/V · Joy · Bliss

The rune of joy, harmony, and the fellowship of the clan. Wunjo represents the deep contentment that arises when one is aligned with their true path and at home among their people. It is a rune of belonging and of wishes fulfilled.

Keywords: Joy, harmony, wish fulfilment, belonging, light, fellowship

Heimdall's Ætt — Runes 9–16

ᚺ

Hagalaz

H · Hail · Disruption

Hailstones — sudden, uncontrollable, destructive, and yet necessary. Hagalaz is the rune of elemental disruption, sudden change, and crisis that ultimately clears the way for growth. The most feared rune in divination, but also a herald of liberation.

Keywords: Disruption, hail, elemental force, sudden change, crisis, purification
ᚾ

Nauthiz

N · Need · Constraint

The fire born of necessity. Nauthiz represents need, constraint, and the friction that generates inner strength. The ancients made fire by rubbing two sticks together — necessity as the mother of power. This rune teaches that hardship contains hidden gifts.

Keywords: Need, constraint, necessity, endurance, shadow work, inner fire
ᛁ

Isaz

I · Ice · Stillness

Ice — pure, still, immovable. Isaz is the rune of standstill, concentration, and the power of waiting. It appears when forward motion is impossible or inadvisable, calling for patience, self-discipline, and introspection.

Keywords: Ice, stillness, standstill, concentration, patience, self-mastery
ᛃ

Jera

J/Y · Year · Harvest

The completed cycle of the year and the harvest earned by patient effort. Jera is one of the most positive runes — it assures that right action over time yields abundant results. A rune of good timing, cyclical renewal, and the reward of perseverance.

Keywords: Harvest, cycle, reward, patience, seasons, natural law
ᛇ

Eihwaz

Ei · Yew Tree · Axis

The yew tree — ancient, poisonous, and immortal, the tree of death and rebirth. Eihwaz is the axis of the world, linked to Yggdrasil itself. It governs endurance, protection, the mysteries of life and death, and access to other realms.

Keywords: Yew tree, life-death cycle, endurance, Yggdrasil, protection, shamanic vision
ᛈ

Perthro

P · Lot-Cup · Fate

One of the most mysterious runes, Perthro is associated with the cup or vessel used for casting lots — the original rune oracle. It governs fate, chance, hidden knowledge, and the wyrd (the web of destiny). It speaks of secrets yet to be revealed.

Keywords: Fate, chance, mystery, hidden things, divination, the Norns
ᛉ

Algiz

Z/R · Elk · Protection

The sedge grass, the elk's antler, the raised hand of protection. Algiz is perhaps the most powerful protective rune, forming an energetic shield. It also signifies connection to the divine — the human figure reaching upward toward the gods.

Keywords: Protection, divine connection, guardian, defence, higher self, sanctuary
ᛊ

Sowilo

S · Sun · Victory

The sun — radiant, victorious, life-giving. Sowilo is the rune of solar power, success, wholeness, and the will to achieve. It illuminates all darkness and is associated with the solar force that drives heroes and warriors toward glory.

Keywords: Sun, victory, will, wholeness, success, clarity, life force

Tyr's Ætt — Runes 17–24

ᛏ

Tiwaz

T · Tyr · Justice

Named for Tyr, the one-handed god of justice who sacrificed his hand to bind the wolf Fenrir for the good of all. Tiwaz is the rune of honour, self-sacrifice, justice, and regulated conflict. The warrior's rune — carved on swords to ensure victory in fair battle.

Keywords: Justice, honour, sacrifice, victory, Tyr, warrior spirit, law
ᛒ

Berkana

B · Birch · Birth

The birch tree — the first to leaf in spring, symbol of new beginnings, nurture, and the great mother. Berkana governs birth, growth, fertility, and the tender care of what is newly begun. It is the rune of the healer and the mother.

Keywords: Birth, growth, nurture, fertility, healing, new beginnings, the feminine
ᛖ

Ehwaz

E · Horse · Partnership

The horse and rider — the perfect partnership between two distinct beings moving as one. Ehwaz governs cooperation, trust, symbiosis, and swift progress. In shamanic practice, the horse is a psychopomp — the vehicle of the soul's journeys between worlds.

Keywords: Partnership, trust, swift progress, horse, soul journeys, cooperation
ᛗ

Mannaz

M · Man · Humanity

The rune of humanity itself — the self in relation to others, the divine in the human form. Mannaz calls for self-reflection, cooperation, and the acknowledgement of our shared nature. It is the rune of the examined life.

Keywords: Humanity, self, introspection, community, the divine human, cooperation
ᛚ

Laguz

L · Lake · Flow

Water — flowing, deep, formless, and all-encompassing. Laguz governs the unconscious, intuition, psychic ability, and the ebb and flow of emotions. It calls for surrender to the current rather than resistance, trusting the unseen depths.

Keywords: Water, intuition, the unconscious, flow, psychic ability, dreams
ᛜ

Ingwaz

Ng · Ing · Fertility

Named for the god Ing (Freyr), deity of fertility, peace, and the sacred harvest. Ingwaz is the rune of completion, stored potential, and the fertile seed held in stillness before its release. It marks a cycle of gestation nearing its culmination.

Keywords: Fertility, potential, completion, Freyr, inner gestation, release
ᛞ

Dagaz

D · Day · Awakening

The moment of dawn — the perfect balance and union of light and dark, and the breakthrough that occurs at their threshold. Dagaz is the rune of awakening, transformation, and the clarity that arrives after a long period of darkness.

Keywords: Dawn, awakening, breakthrough, transformation, balance, enlightenment
ᛟ

Othala

O · Estate · Inheritance

The ancestral home, the inherited land, the bloodline. Othala is the final rune of the Elder Futhark — a rune of completion, heritage, and the wealth of lineage. It speaks of the gifts passed down through family, culture, and tradition, and of finding one's true home.

Keywords: Ancestry, inheritance, home, tradition, clan, completion, belonging

✦ How to Work with Runes

Choosing Your Rune Set

Rune sets are traditionally made from natural materials — wood (especially elder, yew, oak, or ash), stone, bone, or clay. The material matters: natural substances carry their own energies and connect you to the earth from which the rune tradition emerged. Many practitioners carve and colour their own set, which is considered the most powerful approach, as the act of creation attunes you deeply to each character.

When purchasing a set, take time to hold the pieces and sense their resonance. Store your runes in a cloth pouch or wooden box, keep them on or near your person or altar, and handle them often. Before first use, cleanse them — by moonlight, in earth, with smoke from herbs, or by your own breath and intention.

A blank rune tile, sometimes called the Wyrd rune, is included in many modern sets — representing the unknowable, the void, and the fate not yet written. Its use is a modern addition and not part of the historical Elder Futhark.

Creating Sacred Space

Before a reading, take time to ground and centre yourself. Traditional practice involved facing north — the direction of the cosmic axis in Norse cosmology — and calling upon Odin or the Norns (the weavers of fate) to guide the reading.

Spread a white or natural-coloured cloth — called a casting cloth — on a flat surface. This defines the sacred boundary of the reading. Hold your rune bag in both hands, breathe deeply, and formulate your question clearly. A focused, sincere question opens the channel to genuine insight.

Keep a rune journal. Record your readings, your emotional state, and what unfolds in the days following — this is the most powerful way to deepen your relationship with the runes over time.

Common Casting Methods

⬜

Single Rune Draw

The simplest and most powerful method. Draw one rune from the bag without looking. Use for daily guidance, meditation focus, or a direct answer to a single clear question. Meditate on the rune's essence for several minutes before consulting its meaning.

▽

Three Rune Spread

Draw three runes and lay them left to right. The classic layout is Past · Present · Future, though variations include Situation · Action · Outcome, or Challenge · Advice · Resolution. Three-rune spreads offer depth without overwhelming complexity.

✦

The Norns' Spread

Three runes drawn for the three weavers of fate — Urd (what was), Verdandi (what is), and Skuld (what shall be). A deeper version of the three-rune spread, explicitly invoking the Norse concept of wyrd and the interwoven nature of past, present, and becoming.

✚

Five Rune Cross

Five runes placed in a cross pattern — centre (the heart of the matter), above (what aids), below (the foundation), left (the past influencing now), right (the likely outcome). Good for complex situations requiring a holistic view.

🌊

Runic Casting

The oldest method: hold all runes in your hands, focus on your question, and cast them onto the cloth. Read only those that land face-up. Runes near the centre hold immediate relevance; those at the edges speak to peripheral influences. Runes that cross indicate tension.

🔮

Reversed Runes (Merkstave)

When a rune falls upside-down it is called merkstave — "dark stick." It does not mean disaster, but rather the blocked, delayed, or shadow aspect of the rune's energy. Not all runes have distinguishable reversals; those that are symmetrical carry the same meaning in all orientations.

Rune Meditation & Galdr

Runic Meditation

  1. Choose a rune relevant to an area of your life you wish to explore or strengthen.
  2. Sit comfortably, hold the rune tile, and gaze at the symbol with a soft, open focus.
  3. Close your eyes and visualise the rune glowing before you — trace its shape in your mind.
  4. Allow images, feelings, and impressions to arise without forcing them. Simply witness.
  5. Chant or whisper the rune's name slowly and repeatedly — this is the essence of galdr.
  6. Close by expressing gratitude and grounding yourself with deep breaths and awareness of your body.

Runic Inscriptions & Bind Runes

A bind rune (bindrune) is a glyph formed by combining two or more runes into a single symbol — used to concentrate the energies of multiple runes into one focused intention. Medieval Norse craftspeople carved bind runes onto tools, jewellery, and ships for protection and empowerment.

To create a bind rune: identify the runes whose qualities serve your intention, draw them overlapping or sharing lines, and work with the composite image in meditation until it feels alive and unified. Then inscribe it where it will serve — on paper, wood, stone, or skin in ink or henna.

You may also stain rune carvings with red ochre, blood, or red ink — a practice recorded in the Norse sagas. The act of offering something of yourself to the rune deepens the bond between practitioner and symbol.
