Neoplatonism

The great late-antique philosophical tradition — from Plotinus in 3rd-century Rome through Proclus in 5th-century Athens — whose vision of The One, the Nous, and the World Soul became the metaphysical backbone of Western esotericism, Christian theology, and Islamic philosophy for over a millennium.

What Is Neoplatonism?

Neoplatonism is the philosophical tradition founded by Plotinus (205–270 CE) and his student Porphyry, who systematised and deepened the insights of Plato (428–347 BCE). It is called "Neo"-Platonism by modern scholars — the ancients simply called themselves Platonists. Plotinus's system centres on three fundamental hypostases (levels of reality):

  • ◈ The One (τὸ Ἕν) — the absolutely transcendent first principle, beyond all being and thought, the source of all that is. No predicate can be legitimately applied to it; it surpasses even the concept of "existence."
  • ◈ Nous (Νοῦς, Divine Intellect) — the first emanation from the One, the realm of pure thought thinking itself, containing all the Platonic Forms in their eternal, intelligible perfection.
  • ◈ World Soul (Ψυχή) — emanating from Nous, the living principle animating the cosmos, connecting the intelligible and material worlds. The human soul is a particular expression of the World Soul.

All of existence is understood as emanating (like light from the sun) from The One, and everything strives to return to its source — a process Plotinus calls epistrophe (return) or henosis (union).

Key distinction: Neoplatonism is not a creation theology but an emanation theology. The cosmos does not result from a deliberate act of will (as in Genesis) but from the necessary overflow of perfection — the One is so full it overflows without diminishing itself, like a light that illuminates without being depleted.

Matter, at the far end of the chain of emanation, is not evil but a kind of absence — the privation of form and light. The human soul is a World Soul fragment, embodied in matter but retaining its connection to Nous and, through Nous, to The One.

Philosophical practice — dialectic, virtue, and contemplation — enables the soul to ascend through these levels and achieve henosis, mystical union with the One. Plotinus describes such experiences in the Enneads with extraordinary, almost clinical precision.

Theurgy — ritual practice that assists the soul's ascent through divine intermediaries — was emphasised by the Syrian philosopher Iamblichus, who argued that philosophical reasoning alone, however refined, was insufficient for genuine union with the divine.

The Great Neoplatonists

🏛️

Plotinus

205–270 CE · Rome

Egyptian-born, educated in Alexandria under Ammonius Saccas, Plotinus settled in Rome where he taught philosophy for 26 years. His lectures were collected and edited by his student Porphyry as the Enneads — 54 treatises arranged in six groups of nine, among the most profound philosophical texts of antiquity.

Plotinus refused to have his portrait painted, saying his physical appearance was merely "an image of an image." His recorded death words: "I was waiting for you, before the divine principle in me departs to integrate itself with the divine principle of all things."

📜

Porphyry

234–305 CE · Tyre

Student, editor, and biographer of Plotinus. Porphyry compiled and organised the Enneads into their present form, with his famous Life of Plotinus as a preface. His Isagoge (Introduction to Aristotle's Categories) became the standard medieval logic textbook, studied for over a thousand years.

His work Against the Christians survives only in fragments due to imperial suppression. A committed advocate of vegetarianism and physical purification as aids to philosophical ascent.

⚗️

Iamblichus

c. 245–325 CE · Syria

The Syrian philosopher who transformed Neoplatonism by emphasising theurgy (θεουργία — "divine work"). Against Porphyry, Iamblichus argued that philosophical reasoning alone cannot achieve union with the divine; ritual practice — sacrifice, prayer, working with divine statues and symbols — is essential.

His De Mysteriis (On the Mysteries) is the great defence of theurgy in the tradition, and fundamental to everything that follows in late-antique religion and Renaissance magic.

⭐

Proclus

412–485 CE · Athens

Lycian-born, Proclus served as head of the Platonic Academy in Athens and is widely considered the greatest systematic philosopher of late antiquity. His Elements of Theology — a geometrically-structured presentation of Neoplatonic metaphysics in the style of Euclid — is one of the most remarkable works in the history of philosophy.

A deeply religious man who prayed to the sun at dawn and dusk and composed magnificent hymns to the gods. His vast commentaries on Plato, Euclid, and Ptolemy shaped European thought for centuries.

🌙

Damascius

c. 458–538 CE · Damascus

The last head of the Platonic Academy in Athens before the Emperor Justinian closed it in 529 CE. Damascius wrote On the First Principles and the invaluable Life of Isidore. After the Academy's closure, he and six colleagues fled to Persia and the court of Khosrau I — a moment symbolising the end of pagan philosophy within the Roman Empire.

His willingness to push apophatic logic even further than his predecessors — questioning whether "the One" is an adequate description — marks the tradition's most radical extreme.

🔥

Hypatia of Alexandria

c. 360–415 CE · Alexandria

The first well-documented female mathematician and philosopher of note, Hypatia was head of the Neoplatonic school in Alexandria. She taught Plotinus, Porphyry, and Iamblichus to pagans and Christians alike — her students included Synesius of Cyrene, later a Christian bishop who wrote her letters of deep affection.

Murdered by a Christian mob in 415 CE — she was dragged from her chariot, stripped, and killed with pottery shards. Her death is widely taken as a symbol of the destruction of classical learning.

Core Concepts

The One (τὸ Ἕν)

Absolutely simple, beyond all predicates. Not "a being" but the source of Being itself. Cannot be adequately described — any positive statement falls short. Approached by the via negativa (apophatic theology): we can only say what it is not. Beyond thought, beyond Being, beyond goodness — yet the source of all these. Plotinus: to say "the One is" is already too much; to say "the One is one" is almost too much.

Nous (Divine Intellect)

The first emanation from the One. Thinks itself and in doing so generates the Forms (Platonic Ideas). The realm of eternal, unchanging realities: Beauty itself, Justice itself, Being itself. The human intellect participates in Nous and can achieve intellectual vision (noesis) of the Forms — a direct intuition, not discursive reasoning. Nous is the One's first "image."

World Soul (Ψυχή)

Emanates from Nous, mediating between the intelligible and material worlds. Has a higher part always in contact with Nous, and a lower part that generates and animates the material cosmos — producing time, space, and the ordered heavens. The individual human soul is a "particular" expression of the World Soul, retaining a connection upward to Nous even while embodied in matter.

Matter (ὕλη)

Not a principle alongside the One but an absence — the outermost limit of emanation where Being fades into near-non-being. Not evil in itself but the occasion for evil when soul becomes excessively attached to it and forgets its divine origin. Plotinus: matter is "the last and lowest emanation," like shadow at the edge of lamplight.

Emanation

The cosmos proceeds necessarily from the One by a logic of overflow: each level of reality, by virtue of its perfection, "overflows" and generates the next level. This is not a temporal process but an eternal, necessary, simultaneous relationship — as the sun's light is neither before nor after the sun, yet distinct from it. There is no diminishment in the One through emanation.

Theurgy

Ritual practice that assists the soul's ascent through working with divine names, symbols, sacred objects, and statues understood as vehicles of divine power. Iamblichus emphasised that not only the philosopher but the practitioner of sacred ritual can achieve union with the divine. Theurgy bridges the gap between philosophical understanding and lived spiritual transformation.

Henosis — Union with The One

The culmination of the philosophical-contemplative life. Plotinus describes his own experiences of union with the One in the Enneads with rare and beautiful precision — a state beyond subject/object distinction, beyond knower and known, beyond even the awareness of being in union. Not annihilation but the discovery of identity at a level deeper than individual selfhood. Porphyry records that Plotinus achieved this state four times during the years he knew him personally.

A Timeline of Neoplatonism

428 BCE

Plato born in Athens — the philosophical foundation upon which all Neoplatonism would be built.

205 CE

Plotinus born in Egypt — Neoplatonism as a distinct tradition begins with his studies under Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria.

c. 245 CE

Porphyry born in Tyre; Iamblichus born in Syria — two decisive shapers of the tradition's future in Rome and the East respectively.

270 CE

Plotinus dies in Campania. Porphyry subsequently compiles, arranges, and edits the Enneads — 54 treatises in six groups of nine — preserving Plotinus's thought for all time.

331 CE

Julian the Apostate born — the emperor who tried to restore Paganism, deeply shaped by Iamblichan Neoplatonism. His brief reign (361–363 CE) represents the tradition's closest approach to imperial power.

412 CE

Proclus born in Lycia — who would become the great systematiser, head of the Platonic Academy in Athens, and the most technically precise philosopher the tradition produced.

415 CE

Hypatia murdered in Alexandria by a Christian mob — a defining moment in the decline of classical pagan learning.

c. 480 CE

Pseudo-Dionysius writes his four treatises — Neoplatonism systematically "baptised" into Christian theology, ensuring its ideas would survive the fall of pagan institutions.

529 CE

The Emperor Justinian closes the Platonic Academy in Athens. Damascius and six colleagues flee to the court of Khosrau I in Persia — the symbolic end of organised pagan Neoplatonism within the Roman Empire.

800s–900s CE

Arabic transmission flourishes — Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, and later Avicenna develop Islamic Neoplatonism. The "Theology of Aristotle" (actually Plotinus's Enneads IV–VI, paraphrased in Arabic) circulates widely and profoundly shapes Islamic philosophical theology.

1462 & 1484 CE

Marsilio Ficino translates the Corpus Hermeticum (1462) and Plotinus's Enneads (1484) for Cosimo de' Medici in Florence — Neoplatonism re-enters the Latin West and becomes the heart of Renaissance Hermeticism and Humanism.

Neoplatonism's Legacy

Christian Theology

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500 CE) systematically Christianised Neoplatonism: the divine darkness, the hierarchies of angels, and the via negativa (God is beyond all names and categories) all derive directly from Proclus and Plotinus. Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE) encountered Neoplatonism — almost certainly Plotinus and Porphyry in Marius Victorinus's Latin translations — before his conversion, and his Confessions and City of God remain deeply Neoplatonic in structure and sensibility.

Islamic Philosophy

Al-Farabi, Avicenna (Ibn Sina), and Al-Ghazali all worked within a broadly Neoplatonic framework. Avicenna's emanation cosmology — the Active Intellect as the tenth emanation from God, illuminating human intellects — is directly Plotinian. The "Theology of Aristotle" circulated through the Islamic world as if it were Aristotle's own work, ensuring Plotinus's ideas shaped the mainstream of Islamic theology.

Jewish Thought

Maimonides' negative theology — God can only be described by what He is not, since all positive predicates are inadequate — is directly Neoplatonic in inspiration. Medieval Kabbalah shows Neoplatonic influence in its emanation theology: the sefirot as successive emanations from Ein Sof (the Infinite, beyond all determination) follow precisely the Plotinian logic of overflow.

Renaissance Hermeticism

Marsilio Ficino's synthesis of Plato, Plotinus, Hermes Trismegistus, and Christianity — carried out in the Platonic Academy of Florence under Cosimo de' Medici's patronage — is the heart of Renaissance esotericism. Ficino translated the complete Enneads and wrote his Theologia Platonica in conscious imitation of Proclus. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, Giordano Bruno, and the entire Hermetic-Cabalist tradition owe Neoplatonism an immense debt.

German Idealism

Hegel, Schelling, and to some extent Fichte were deeply influenced by Neoplatonic ideas about Absolute Spirit and dialectical return. Hegel's triad of Being-Nothing-Becoming echoes the Plotinian triad of One-Nous-Soul. Schelling's Ages of the World draws directly on Böhme, who himself drew on late Neoplatonism.

Modern Esotericism

The entire Western esoteric tradition of planes of existence, emanation cosmology, and mystical ascent owes an enormous debt to Neoplatonism. Theosophy (Blavatsky), the Golden Dawn, Anthroposophy (Steiner), and virtually every modern occult cosmology recycles the Plotinian schema of descending and ascending levels of reality.

Contemplative Practice

Plotinus describes the practice of philosophical contemplation in the Enneads with great precision. The key practice unfolds in stages: first, an internal withdrawal from sensory distraction and the concerns of the physical body; then a rise to the level of Soul — attending to the soul's own activity rather than its objects; from Soul to Nous — seeing all things in their eternal, ideal forms, no longer as separate impressions but as a unified intelligible whole; and from Nous toward The One — an act of total simplification, a radical interior stillness, where even the distinction of knower and known dissolves into pure presence.

Plotinus famously instructs: "Reduce everything to the One and the One to nothing." Henosis, when it comes, is not achieved by effort but by a kind of graceful relaxation of the separating self — the soul "opens" and the One, which was always already present, fills the space vacated by self-concern.

The Enneads as spiritual manual: Reading Plotinus slowly and meditatively — as the philosopher Pierre Hadot recommends in his Philosophy as a Way of Life — is itself a contemplative practice. Hadot showed convincingly that ancient philosophy was not primarily a theoretical enterprise but a set of spiritual exercises: daily practices of attention, reflection, and transformation. To read Plotinus in this spirit is to engage with one of the most powerful contemplative technologies in the Western tradition.

Iamblichan theurgy: For those who find pure philosophy insufficient, the ritual path provides a complement. Iamblichus maintained that the gods respond to sacred ritual because it operates at the level of the divine order itself — sacred statues, divine names, and ritual actions are not human conventions but genuine expressions of the divine within the material world. Theurgic practice thus meets the divine "from below," mobilising the divine content embedded in material creation.

"Often I have woken to myself out of the body — become external to all other things and internal to myself — beholding a marvellous beauty, and then above all convinced that I belong to the better part, having the best of life lived to the full, become one with the divine." — Plotinus, Enneads IV.8.1
