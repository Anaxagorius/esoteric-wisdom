Zoroastrianism

The ancient Persian religion of Ahura Mazda — one of the world's oldest revealed faiths, shaped by the prophet-poet Zarathustra and still practised today by the Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian communities in a continuous tradition stretching back more than three millennia.

Introduction

Zarathustra (Greek: Zoroaster) is one of the most historically significant yet enigmatic figures in the history of religion. A priest of the ancient Iranian religion who received, according to the Gathas (the hymns he composed), a direct visionary experience of Ahura Mazda — the Wise Lord — Zarathustra's revelation introduced into the world a number of ideas that would prove permanently transformative: a single supreme good deity opposed by an equally powerful cosmic adversary; a linear history moving toward a final renovation of the world (Frashokereti); individual moral responsibility and the judgement of the soul after death; and the concept of the Saoshyant — a saviour figure who would appear at the end of time to defeat evil and raise the dead. Scholars debate his dates intensely, with estimates ranging from 1500 BCE to 600 BCE, but his influence on subsequent religious history is beyond dispute.

The sacred scripture of Zoroastrianism — the Avesta — is preserved only partially; much was lost during Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia and the subsequent Hellenistic period. The surviving texts include the Gathas (Zarathustra's own hymns, composed in a very ancient form of Iranian), the Yasna (liturgical texts), the Yashts (hymns to specific divine beings), and the Vendidad (purity laws). The Gathas are among the oldest religious poetry in the world and present a vision of cosmic ethical struggle — between Asha (Truth/Righteousness) and Druj (the Lie) — of extraordinary power and clarity. Every human being, by their thoughts, words, and deeds, participates in this cosmic battle: to think good thoughts, speak good words, and do good deeds is to actively strengthen the forces of Truth against the Lie.

The Amesha Spentas — the Holy Immortals — are the six divine emanations or aspects of Ahura Mazda who, together with their creator, form a heptad of divine beings governing the cosmos. They are: Vohu Manah (Good Mind), Asha Vahishta (Best Truth/Righteousness), Khshathra Vairya (Desirable Dominion), Spenta Armaiti (Holy Devotion), Haurvatat (Wholeness), and Ameretat (Immortality). Each Amesha Spenta has a corresponding aspect of the created world under its protection (fire, cattle, metal, earth, water, plants) and a corresponding human virtue it represents. This system of correspondence between divine principles, natural elements, and human qualities is one of the most elegant cosmological architectures in any religious tradition.

The sacred fire (Atar) is the most visible and distinctive element of Zoroastrian practice. Fire temples house sacred fires that have burned continuously for centuries — some for over a thousand years — tended by a hereditary priesthood who approach them wearing mouth-veils so that no human breath may pollute the sacred flame. The fire is understood not merely as a symbol of the divine but as a genuine presence of Ahura Mazda — the son of the creator, one of the most sacred realities in the cosmos. The ancient Iranian fire altar, with its characteristic winged disc symbol (the Faravahar), became one of the most recognisable images of Persian civilisation and continues to be the central symbol of Zoroastrian identity today.

Core Concepts

☀️

Ahura Mazda (Wise Lord)

The supreme deity of Zoroastrianism — the Wise Lord, uncreated, wholly good, omniscient, and the source of all that is true and righteous. Ahura Mazda is not the creator of evil; evil arises from the destructive spirit Angra Mainyu, who exists in opposition to the divine order. The relationship between Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu constitutes the cosmic dualism that is Zoroastrianism's most distinctive theological contribution and its most influential legacy.

🌑

Angra Mainyu (Destructive Spirit)

The adversarial cosmic spirit — also called Ahriman — who chose wrongly at the primal moment of existence and stands in eternal opposition to Ahura Mazda and the order of Truth. Angra Mainyu is responsible for all evil, death, disease, and deception in the world. The cosmos is the arena of their conflict; human beings are the moral agents whose choices determine the outcome. Crucially, Angra Mainyu is not eternal — the Frashokereti (cosmic renovation) will ultimately defeat and dissolve him.

⚖️

Asha (Truth & Order)

The foundational principle of Zoroastrian ethics and cosmology — Truth, Righteousness, Order, Right Working. Asha is simultaneously a cosmic law (the way the universe is rightly ordered), a divine being (one of the Amesha Spentas), and a moral obligation (the standard to which every human action must be measured). Its opposite, Druj (the Lie), encompasses all deception, chaos, and moral corruption. The entire Zoroastrian ethical system flows from the simple injunction to live in accord with Asha.

🔥

Atar (Sacred Fire)

The sacred fire — simultaneously a divine being (son of Ahura Mazda), a cosmic principle (the living light of truth and purity), and a physical reality (the continuously burning fires of Zoroastrian fire temples). Approaching the sacred fire requires ritual purity; the hereditary priests who tend it wear white garments and white mouth-veils, and use long-handled ladles to add sandalwood and frankincense without contaminating the flames. The Atash Bahram (Victory Fire), the highest grade of sacred fire, is kindled from sixteen different fires of different occupations and ritually purified over months.

👼

Fravashi (Divine Guardian)

The pre-existing divine guardian spirit of each person — an eternal spiritual double that existed before birth and continues after death. The Fravashi is not merely the individual soul but a higher spiritual self, a portion of divine being that has chosen to enter the material world to assist in the cosmic battle against evil. The Faravahar symbol — the winged disc with a human figure — is understood by many as a representation of the Fravashi. The Fravashis of the righteous are honoured annually during the Farvardegan festival.

🌉

Chinvat Bridge (The Crossing)

The bridge that every soul must cross at death — broad and easy for the righteous, razor-thin and impossible for the wicked. At the bridge, the soul encounters the daena (its own conscience and accumulated moral nature, appearing as a beautiful maiden to the just and a hideous crone to the wicked), and is judged by the divine beings Mithra, Sraosha, and Rashnu. The righteous pass to paradise (the House of Song); the wicked fall into the House of Lies. This afterlife geography directly influenced later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic eschatology.

The Magi and Star Wisdom

The Magi — the priestly caste of ancient Iranian religion — were among the ancient world's most celebrated masters of astronomy, astrology, dream interpretation, and divination. The Greek word magos (from which we derive "magic" and "magi") originally referred specifically to the Zoroastrian priests of the Median and Persian tradition; their reputation for wisdom, astronomical knowledge, and the interpretation of omens spread throughout the ancient world, making "magi" almost synonymous with "learned man" in classical Greek and Roman literature. It is the Magi of Matthew's Gospel who follow the star to Bethlehem — an episode that reflects the historical reality of Zoroastrian astronomical expertise and the tradition's expectation of a future saviour.

The Zoroastrian contribution to astronomical and astrological knowledge was substantial. The ancient Iranian solar calendar — based on the observation of solstices, equinoxes, and the six seasonal festivals (Gahambars) — reflects centuries of sophisticated astronomical observation. The Magi's reputation for knowledge of "the stars" in the ancient world was well-founded; Iranian astronomy contributed significantly to the transmission of Babylonian astrological knowledge to the Hellenistic world and, through it, to the Western astrological tradition. The concept of the cosmic cycle of world ages — with its four ages of metals (gold, silver, steel, iron) declining from a primal golden age — which appears in Hesiod and Plato, is now widely understood to have entered Greek thought through Iranian influence, whether Zoroastrian or related traditions. The Zoroastrian expectation of three future Saoshyants (saviours), born at thousand-year intervals from the preserved seed of Zarathustra, who will progressively renovate the world and culminate in the final Frashokereti, represents one of the most developed eschatological visions in any ancient tradition.

Influence on Western Tradition

The influence of Zoroastrian ideas on the development of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam has been one of the most productive areas of scholarship in the history of religions. The exilic and post-exilic period of Jewish history (sixth to fifth centuries BCE) saw the Jewish community in Babylon under Persian Achaemenid rule — a period during which several characteristically Zoroastrian ideas appear, for the first time, in Jewish literature: the figure of Satan as a distinct cosmic adversary (rather than merely a member of the divine court); the resurrection of the dead; the final judgement; a linear eschatology moving toward a catastrophic end and renovation; and the concept of angelic beings with individual names and specific functions. These ideas, absorbed from the Zoroastrian environment, became central to the apocalyptic Judaism of the Second Temple period and, through it, to both Christianity and Islam.

The concept of cosmic dualism — the eternal opposition of a good cosmic principle and an evil one — entered the Western esoteric tradition most explicitly through Gnosticism and Manichaeism, both of which drew substantially on Zoroastrian cosmological frameworks. The Manichaean religion (founded by Mani in third-century Mesopotamia) is arguably a synthesis of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Buddhism, and its dualist cosmology was influential enough to constitute a major theological challenge to orthodox Christianity for centuries, demanding the sustained attention of theologians from Tertullian to Augustine. The Cathar movement of medieval southern France represents one of the latest echoes of this Iranian-inspired dualist tradition in Western history. Today, Zoroastrianism survives as a living religion practised by the Parsi community of India (concentrated in Mumbai), the Iranian Zoroastrian community, and diaspora communities worldwide — a small but proud tradition maintaining one of the world's oldest continuously practised religious lineages.

Recommended Reading

📖

Mary Boyce

A History of Zoroastrianism (3 vols., 1975–1991) — the definitive scholarly history of the tradition by the British scholar who spent extended periods living with Zoroastrian communities in Iran to understand the tradition from the inside. Boyce's work is the indispensable academic foundation for the serious study of Zoroastrianism. Her shorter Zoroastrians: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices (1979) is the most accessible single-volume introduction for the general reader.

📚

Geo Widengren

Die Religionen Irans (1965; English translation The Religions of Iran) — the major comparative study of Iranian religious history, covering the pre-Zoroastrian background, the Gathic tradition, the Achaemenid period, and the Sassanid development. Widengren's comparative method and his attention to the relationships between Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and Mandaeism make this work essential for understanding the tradition in its full historical context.

🌿

R.C. Zaehner

The Dawn and Twilight of Zoroastrianism (1961) — Zaehner's classic account of Zoroastrian history and theology, from the Gathas through the Sassanid period and the Islamic conquest. Written with clarity and genuine scholarly passion, it remains one of the most readable introductions to the tradition's intellectual and spiritual depth. Zaehner's broader work on mysticism (Mysticism Sacred and Profane, 1957) places Zoroastrianism within a valuable comparative framework.

🔥

Shaul Shaked

Dualism in Transformation: Varieties of Religion in Sasanian Iran (1994) — a scholarly exploration of the Sassanid period (224–651 CE), during which Zoroastrianism was the state religion of the Persian Empire and reached its fullest institutional development. Shaked's work on the interaction between Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and early Kabbalistic literature is particularly valuable for understanding how Iranian esoteric ideas fed into the broader Western mystical tradition.
