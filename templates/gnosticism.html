Gnosticism

Ancient liberationist religions of the first centuries CE — teaching that this world was made in error by a lesser god, that the human soul carries a divine spark of transcendent light, and that gnosis (direct knowledge of the True God) alone can liberate the spirit from the prison of matter.

What Is Gnosticism?

The word gnosis (γνῶσις) is Greek for "knowledge" — but this is no ordinary intellectual knowledge. It is direct, experiential, transformative knowledge of the divine, qualitatively distinct from mere intellectual knowledge (episteme) or faith (pistis). To have gnosis is not to believe certain propositions about God, nor to understand them philosophically — it is to have encountered the divine reality directly, to have recognised within oneself the spark of transcendent light that one truly is. This distinction is fundamental to understanding everything that follows.

Gnosticism refers to a richly diverse family of religious movements flourishing in the 1st–4th centuries CE across the Mediterranean world, from Egypt and Syria to Rome and Gaul. Despite their enormous diversity in myth, ritual, and theological detail, they shared a cluster of characteristic convictions. First: the material world was not created by the highest, most transcendent God, but by a lower, ignorant, or even malevolent creator being known as the Demiurge (from Greek demiourgos, "craftsman" or "artisan"). Second: the human being contains a divine spark — pneuma (spirit), phos (light), spinthir (spark) — that is entrapped within the material body and the psychic structures of ordinary human life. Third: salvation comes through gnosis — the direct experiential recognition of one's divine origin and of the path by which the divine spark can return to its source.

The tripartite anthropology that recurs across many Gnostic systems divides humanity into three types: the pneumatics (spirit-people, those who possess the divine spark in full measure and are fully capable of gnosis and salvation), the psychics (soul-people, who have a degree of spiritual capacity and may achieve partial salvation through faith and good works, but lack the direct gnosis of the pneumatics), and the hylics (matter-people, those so entirely identified with the material world that they are incapable of the spiritual awakening that constitutes salvation). In many Gnostic systems, orthodox Christians are classified as psychics — well-intentioned but spiritually limited — while the Gnostics themselves are the pneumatics.

The heart of Gnostic theology is the distinction between the Demiurge and the True God. In mainstream Jewish and Christian theology, the creator of the world — YHWH of the Hebrew Bible — is identical with the supreme and entirely good God. In Gnostic theology, this identification is rejected with considerable force. The creator god of Genesis — the god who creates matter, imposes law, demands obedience, and declares "I am a jealous God" — is identified with the Demiurge, a lesser being who created the material world either in ignorance of the higher divine reality or in a deliberate act of arrogance and world-imprisonment.

Above and beyond the Demiurge, entirely transcendent and uninvolved in the material world, is the True God — the Invisible Spirit, the Monad, the First Principle, the Father of All. This God is described in the most emphatic negative theology: beyond all predicates, beyond being itself, unknowable to the ordinary human mind, accessible only through gnosis. The True God did not create the material world and is not responsible for its suffering and limitation.

The Pleroma (πλήρωμα, "fullness") is the divine realm of the True God — the infinite luminous space of divine reality, populated by pairs of divine beings called Aeons, each embodying an attribute of God: Mind, Truth, Logos, Life, Church, and so forth. The drama of Gnostic cosmology begins within the Pleroma itself, with a crisis among the Aeons that leads, through a chain of consequences, to the creation of the material world and the entrapment of the divine sparks within it.

The Sophia myth (the myth of divine Wisdom's fall) is central to many Gnostic systems: Sophia, the youngest and outermost of the divine Aeons, overreaches in her desire to know the Father without her consort. This unilateral, passionate act produces an unformed, abortion-like being — the Demiurge — who is then banished from the Pleroma. Ignorant of his origins, the Demiurge declares himself the only god and creates the material world. But the Pleroma is not without influence: the divine spark — a seed of Sophia's light — is planted within humanity, and through gnosis, these divine sparks can be awakened, liberated, and restored to the Pleroma.

Major Gnostic Schools

🌞

Valentinian Gnosticism

c. 100–400 CE

The most philosophically sophisticated Gnostic school, founded by Valentinus (c. 100–160 CE), who studied in Alexandria and taught in Rome. Valentinus constructed an extraordinarily elaborate Gnostic theology: the Pleroma of 30 Aeons arranged in ogdoads and decads, the drama of Sophia's fall and the creation of the Demiurge from her fallen emotion (enthymesis), the formation of the material world as a vehicle for the eventual restoration of the divine sparks. Valentinus himself was a man of remarkable learning and subtlety — he nearly became Bishop of Rome. His school produced major theologians including Ptolemy, Heracleon, and Theodotus, and their works were extensively quoted and refuted by Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria. The Gospel of Truth found at Nag Hammadi is almost certainly by Valentinus himself — a poetic, meditative homily of profound beauty.

♈

Sethian Gnosticism

c. 1st–3rd century CE

An early and radically mythological Gnostic current centred on Seth, the third son of Adam, as a divine saviour figure, prototype of the pneumatic soul, and recipient of gnosis in each age. The key feminine divine figure is Barbelo — the first and greatest of the divine emanations, the "thrice-male Mother", the divine self-reflection of the Invisible Spirit. Sethian texts represent some of the oldest and most technically sophisticated Gnostic theology. Their key works include the Apocryphon of John (the Secret Book of John), the Gospel of the Egyptians, Allogenes, and Zostrianos. Strikingly, several of these texts were known to the Neoplatonist philosopher Plotinus, who critiqued Sethian Gnostic cosmology in his Enneads — evidence that Gnosticism was engaging seriously with the highest philosophical circles of late antiquity.

🐍

Ophite Gnosticism

c. 2nd century CE

The Ophites (from Greek ophis, "serpent") are among the most provocatively counter-cultural of the Gnostic groups. In their reading of Genesis, the serpent in the Garden of Eden is not the tempter of humanity but the emissary of the True God, bringing gnosis to humanity against the Demiurge's prohibition. The command "Do not eat of the tree of knowledge" was the Demiurge's instruction to keep humanity in ignorance and subservience. The serpent, by urging humanity to eat, was performing an act of liberation — offering humanity the divine gnosis that the Demiurge wished to withhold. This radical inversion of the biblical narrative provoked horror and fascination among Church Fathers. In some Ophite systems, the serpent was literally venerated in ritual, leading to accusations of snake worship.

💧

Mandaean Gnosticism

c. 1st–2nd century CE – present

The only surviving ancient Gnostic religion, the Mandaeans number approximately 100,000 today, primarily in Iraq, Iran, and diaspora communities in Europe, Australia, and North America. Their origins lie in 1st–2nd century CE Jordan or southern Mesopotamia, and they have maintained an unbroken religious tradition pre-dating Islam. The name Mandaean derives from manda, meaning "knowledge" (gnosis). Their central divine figure is Manda d-Hiia — the "Knowledge of Life" — who descends into the material world to bring saving gnosis. Ritual baptism (masbuta), performed repeatedly in flowing water for spiritual purification, is central to Mandaean practice. John the Baptist is revered as a major prophet; Jesus, by contrast, is viewed as a false messiah who distorted John's teachings. The Mandaean sacred texts, the Ginza Rba (Great Treasure), are among the most important Gnostic primary sources.

☀

Manichaeism

Founded c. 240 CE

Founded by the Persian prophet Mani (216–274 CE) in the Sassanid Empire, Manichaeism was one of the most successful missionary religions in history — at its height spreading from the Atlantic coast of Spain to the Tang Dynasty court of China. Mani synthesised Zoroastrian cosmic dualism (the eternal conflict of Light and Darkness as two co-equal principles), Gnostic Christianity (with a distinctive Docetist Christology), and Buddhist ethical and monastic elements into a unified religion of cosmic scope. The basic Manichaean myth describes how particles of divine Light became trapped in matter through a primordial cosmic battle, and how the task of the elect (the Hearers and the Elect) is to release this light through ethical living, prayer, and the avoidance of harm. Augustine of Hippo was a Manichaean adherent for nine years before his conversion to Christianity — and many scholars argue Manichaean patterns of thought continued to influence his theology of grace and sin.

Sacred Gnostic Texts

📚 Nag Hammadi Library

In December 1945, near the Upper Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi, a local farmer named Mohammed Ali al-Samman discovered a large sealed ceramic jar buried near a cliff face. Inside were twelve bound codices and a loose leaf, containing 52 tractates written in Coptic (the late-stage Egyptian language written in the Greek alphabet). This was the greatest single discovery of Gnostic texts in history — transforming modern understanding of both early Christianity and the Gnostic movement. The library was buried around 390 CE, probably by monks from the nearby Pachomian monastery attempting to preserve texts condemned by the increasingly orthodox Church. The complete English translation, edited by James Robinson, was published in 1977 as The Nag Hammadi Library in English.

📖 Gospel of Thomas

One of the most studied and debated early Christian texts, the Gospel of Thomas consists of 114 sayings of the "living Jesus" with no narrative framework — no birth, no miracles, no crucifixion, no resurrection. Many sayings have clear parallels in the synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke), while others are unique to Thomas and profoundly Gnostic in character. The incipit reads: "These are the secret sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymos Judas Thomas recorded." The famous saying 3 states: "The Kingdom is inside you and it is outside you." Saying 56: "Whoever has come to know the world has discovered a carcass, and whoever has discovered a carcass, of that person the world is not worthy." The question of whether Thomas contains authentic early Jesus traditions independent of the synoptic Gospels remains one of the most contested issues in New Testament scholarship.

✍️ Gospel of Truth

Found at Nag Hammadi, almost certainly composed by Valentinus himself (c. 140–160 CE), the Gospel of Truth is not a Gospel in the narrative sense but a meditative theological homily of extraordinary poetic power. It meditates on the nature of error (plane) as a fog that concealed the Father, on the anguish of ignorance, and on the joy of gnosis as the recognition of the Father's love. "The Gospel of Truth is joy for those who have received from the Father of Truth the grace of knowing him." It reveals Valentinus as a theologian of genuine depth and beauty, far from the crude caricature presented by his opponents.

❤️ Gospel of Philip

A Valentinian Gnostic compilation of diverse theological reflections, meditations, and sayings, the Gospel of Philip became famous in popular culture for its passage describing Jesus kissing Mary Magdalene on the mouth — a passage that, read in its Gnostic sacramental context, refers to the transmission of gnosis through the sacred kiss rather than any romantic relationship. The text develops a sophisticated Gnostic sacramental theology built around five rites: baptism, chrism (anointing), eucharist, redemption, and the "bridal chamber" — the highest sacrament, representing the reunion of the soul with its divine counterpart and ultimately with God.

👑 Pistis Sophia

A lengthy Gnostic text probably composed in the 3rd or early 4th century CE, the Pistis Sophia presents elaborate dialogues between the risen Christ and his disciples — particularly Mary Magdalene, who speaks more than any other disciple and is described as the one "whose heart is more directed to the Kingdom of Heaven than all thy brethren." The text presents Sophia's fall into the material realm and her rescue by Christ through an intricate hymnic and mythological narrative, together with complex teachings on the soul's journey through the afterlife and the mechanics of cosmic purification.

🔸 Secret Book of John (Apocryphon of John)

Found in four separate versions (two longer, two shorter) at Nag Hammadi and in the Berlin Codex, the Apocryphon of John is the single most important Sethian Gnostic text — presenting the most complete account of Sethian cosmogony. The resurrected Christ appears to John the son of Zebedee and reveals the complete Gnostic cosmological drama: the description of the Invisible Spirit (the Monad), the emanation of Barbelo, the creation of the self-willed Demiurge Yaldabaoth (described as a lion-headed serpent), his declaration "I am a jealous God; there is no other God but me," the creation of the material world and of humanity, the planting of the divine spark within Adam, and the path of liberation through gnosis.

The Gnostic Cosmology

The Gnostic universe unfolds in a series of stages descending from absolute perfection to absolute imperfection — from the transcendent divine realm to the prison of matter. Understanding this cosmological structure is essential to understanding the Gnostic path of liberation.

At the absolute summit of reality stands the Monad — the True Father, the Invisible Spirit, the First Principle. In the most thoroughgoing negative theology, the Gnostics insist that no predicate can be accurately applied to this ultimate reality: it is not being, for being is a category of created existence; it is not good in any sense we can comprehend; it is not mind, not spirit, not any finite attribute. It simply is — the ineffable, incomprehensible Source. And yet, in a profound paradox, it is this unknowable Ground from which all knowledge ultimately comes, and toward which gnosis ultimately leads.

From the Monad there proceeds a luminous realm: the Pleroma (the divine Fullness), a realm of Aeons — divine beings or attributes of God, existing in syzygy pairs (male-female complementary pairs): Mind and Truth, Logos (Word) and Life, Church and Humanity, and many others. The Pleroma is the home of the divine sparks before their fall, the realm to which they must return.

The catastrophe begins with Sophia — the youngest and outermost Aeon, divine Wisdom. In her passionate desire to know the Father directly, without the mediation of her consort, she produces an unformed being — an abortion-like emanation that cannot be received into the Pleroma. This being is the Demiurge, called in different Gnostic texts Yaldabaoth (a name of uncertain meaning, perhaps "child of chaos"), Saklas (the Aramaic word for "fool"), or Samael (the "blind god"). He is given a leonine or serpentine form in many traditions — powerful but spiritually blind, the image of material power without spiritual wisdom.

The Demiurge, utterly ignorant of the Pleroma from which he fell, gazes at his own reflection in the watery abyss below and mistakes himself for the supreme God. His declaration from Exodus 20:5 — "I am a jealous God; there is no other God but me" — is read by the Gnostics as the definitive proof of his ignorance: the True God has no jealousy, no rivals, and no need to assert his supremacy. The Demiurge then creates seven subordinate rulers — the Archons (from Greek archon, "ruler") — who govern the seven planetary spheres. Together, the Demiurge and his Archons create the material world and the human body as a prison for the divine sparks.

Yet the Pleroma intervenes. Through Sophia's repentance and the action of the higher divine powers, the divine spark is planted within the human being — specifically within Adam — through the breath of life (pneuma). This act, described in the Apocryphon of John with remarkable dramatic power, transforms the situation: now the material world contains, hidden within its most elevated creature, a fragment of the divine light that the Demiurge himself could not have created.

The Gnostic path is, therefore, fundamentally a path of recognition: recognising within oneself the divine spark, understanding its origin and its entrapment, acquiring the gnosis of the divine realm and of the path of return, and through gnosis and correct ritual practice (baptism, chrism, the bridal chamber sacrament), learning to navigate past the Archons at death and achieve the final return to the Pleroma. The Archons, at the moment of death, demand that the soul "render an account" and attempt to retain it in the cycle of material existence. The Gnostic, armed with gnosis and the correct ritual passwords, passes through each planetary sphere unhindered, shedding the material qualities acquired at each level, and returns to the divine Fullness.

Key Figures

💎

Valentinus

c. 100–160 CE — Alexandria & Rome

The greatest Gnostic theologian. Born in Egypt and educated in Alexandria, Valentinus moved to Rome around 136 CE, where he taught for two decades and was, according to Tertullian, seriously considered for the position of Bishop of Rome. He founded the most philosophically sophisticated Gnostic school, and the surviving fragments of his own writing reveal a thinker of exceptional subtlety, poetic sensitivity, and theological depth. His school produced a generation of remarkable theologians who engaged seriously with Platonism, Stoicism, and orthodox Christianity on equal terms.

🔹

Basilides

c. 117–138 CE — Alexandria

An Alexandrian Gnostic teacher who constructed one of the most technically elaborate Gnostic cosmologies: a cosmic hierarchy of 365 heavens, each governed by its own set of angelic powers, with the God of the Hebrew Bible occupying the lowest of these heavens. Basilides claimed to derive his teaching from Glaucias, an interpreter of St Peter. Both Clement of Alexandria and Origen engaged with his ideas with a seriousness that suggests he was a figure of real intellectual stature. His cosmological system, though known only through the hostile reports of his opponents, reveals a thinker grappling deeply with the problem of evil and the relationship between divine transcendence and material existence.

📚

Marcion of Sinope

c. 85–160 CE — Pontus & Rome

Technically not a Gnostic in the full sense (he rejected mythological cosmology and the concept of the divine spark), Marcion nevertheless represents the most radical expression of the Gnostic impulse: the absolute rejection of the Hebrew Bible's God as incompatible with the God revealed by Jesus. Marcion created the first Christian scriptural canon — an edited version of the Gospel of Luke and ten Pauline letters, stripped of all Old Testament quotation — and thereby inadvertently forced the mainstream Church to define and close its own canon. His challenge to the Church was so profound that Tertullian wrote five books against him. He remains one of the most consequential and controversial figures in the history of Christianity.

☀

Mani

216–274 CE — Persia

Born in Babylon to a family belonging to the Jewish-Christian baptismal sect of the Elchasaites, Mani received visionary revelations at the ages of 12 and 24 that impelled him to found a new universal religion. He understood himself as the "Seal of the Prophets" — the final, definitive prophet completing and synthesising the work of Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus. He created not only a theology but a complete religious organisation, a body of sacred scriptures (illustrated with paintings), and a missionary network that spread his teaching across the known world. Executed by the Zoroastrian Magi under King Bahram I, Mani became a martyr-prophet whose religion survived him by over a millennium in Central Asia and China.

⚡

Simon Magus

1st century CE — Samaria & Rome

Mentioned in Acts 8 as a Samaritan magician who attempted to purchase the power of the Holy Spirit from Peter (giving rise to the term "simony"), Simon Magus became the legendary prototype of the Gnostic teacher-magician in later tradition. The Church Fathers regarded him as the "father of all heresies." In later Gnostic sources, Simon appears as a divine being who descended to earth to liberate his consort Helen (Ennoia) — the divine Thought of God — who had fallen into matter and was trapped in a cycle of reincarnation, most recently as a prostitute in the city of Tyre. The Simon and Helen mythology represents one of the earliest complete Gnostic redemption narratives.

Gnosticism Today

Gnosticism never entirely disappeared from Western culture, but the 20th century saw a remarkable revival on multiple fronts: academic, psychological, literary, and religious.

In the realm of living religious practice, several Neo-Gnostic churches continue the Gnostic tradition in contemporary form. The most prominent is the Ecclesia Gnostica, founded in Los Angeles by Bishop Stephan Hoeller — a Hungarian-born scholar and practitioner who has been a major force in both Gnostic scholarship and practice for over half a century. The Apostolic Johannite Church, the Ecclesia Gnostica Catholica (the ecclesiastical arm of Thelema), and various independent Gnostic communities continue to celebrate Gnostic sacramental liturgy and maintain the tradition as a living spiritual path.

The great Swiss psychologist Carl Gustav Jung engaged profoundly with Gnosticism throughout his life. In 1916, in a state of psychic crisis, he produced the Septem Sermones ad Mortuos (Seven Sermons to the Dead) — a visionary Gnostic text he attributed to the Gnostic teacher Basilides of Alexandria. Jung later identified the Gnostic myths as ancient expressions of the archetypes of the collective unconscious: the Demiurge as the Shadow (the unconscious, unintegrated aspect of the psyche), the Pleroma as the totality of the unconscious, the Sophia myth as the drama of the anima's fall and redemption, and individuation (the integration of the whole self) as the psychological equivalent of gnosis. His Answer to Job (1952) represents his most sustained theological engagement with the Gnostic critique of the Hebrew God.

The American science-fiction writer Philip K. Dick (1928–1982) experienced what he called a "theophanic" revelation in February-March 1974 — a prolonged visionary encounter with what he identified as a divine intelligence he called VALIS (Vast Active Living Intelligence System). Spending the remaining eight years of his life attempting to understand this experience, Dick produced the vast Exegesis (approximately 8,000 pages of private journal entries) and the VALIS trilogy. His conclusions were strikingly Gnostic: this world is a kind of prison or illusion maintained by a Demiurgic power, the true divine intelligence (the Logos) is hidden within reality and can break through to those capable of receiving it, and the task of the awakened individual is to recognise the true nature of reality and cooperate with the salvific action of the Logos.

The literary critic Harold Bloom argued controversially that Gnosticism — with its emphasis on the individual's direct, unmediated encounter with the divine and its suspicion of institutional authority — is not an aberration from but the authentic expression of the American religious imagination. The scholar Elaine Pagels brought Gnostic scholarship to a wide popular audience with her landmark work The Gnostic Gospels (1979), demonstrating the diversity and vitality of early Christianity before the imposition of orthodoxy. Scholars including Kurt Rudolph, Bentley Layton, and April DeConick continue to develop rigorous academic understanding of the Gnostic traditions. The discovery of the Gospel of Judas in the 2000s and its publication in 2006 reminded the world that Gnostic Christianity still has surprises to offer.
