Ascension / Illumination

The telos of the esoteric path: the elevation of consciousness beyond the ordinary human condition to a state of divine awareness — whether called illumination, enlightenment, deification, union, or ascension, this is the culmination that every authentic esoteric tradition moves toward.

What Is Ascension / Illumination?

The Orthodox Christian doctrine of theosis — deification or divinisation — is among the most radically ambitious spiritual goals articulated by any of the world's religious traditions. The formula attributed to Athanasius of Alexandria — "God became human so that humans might become God" — encapsulates the theotic vision with memorable brevity: the purpose of the divine Incarnation is not primarily the remission of past sins but the opening of a path by which human beings can participate in the divine nature and ultimately become, by grace and participation, what God is by nature. The crucial qualification — "by grace, not by nature" — distinguishes the Orthodox doctrine from the pantheistic identification of human and divine: the deified human being is not absorbed into the divine essence, not merged with God in a way that dissolves human individuality, but transformed so thoroughly by divine grace that they participate in the divine life, share the divine knowledge, and radiate the divine love, while remaining irreducibly themselves. The distinctions between divine essence (inaccessible) and divine energies (fully communicable) developed by Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth century provide the metaphysical framework within which this paradox can be articulated: the human being can genuinely become divine by participating fully in the divine energies, without this participation implying a merger with the divine essence that would be ontologically impossible.

Plotinus's account of the mystical union with the One — scattered through the Enneads and concentrated in the treatise VI.9 — is the most philosophically rigorous description of ascension and illumination in the Western philosophical tradition. The soul, for Plotinus, ascends through successive stages of purification and contemplation — from the sensible world to the rational world, from the rational world to the noetic world of pure Intellect, and from Intellect to the threshold of the One — before finally, in the most intimate and exceptional of experiences, being joined to the One in a union that transcends all predication and all cognitive duality. This union is not a permanent state — Plotinus records that he experienced it only "four times" in his life — but each experience leaves a permanent trace: the soul that has touched the One even once cannot entirely forget it, and the memory of the encounter serves as an indelible orientation for all subsequent spiritual life. The language Plotinus uses for this supreme experience — the "flight of the alone to the Alone," the "simplification" of the soul, the "touching" of the One — is carefully chosen to avoid claiming more than the experience permits: it is a genuine contact, not a confusion of identities; a union, not a merger.

The Sufi tradition of Islam has developed perhaps the most elaborate systematic account of the stages of spiritual ascent available in any contemplative tradition. The maqamat — "stations" — are stable achievements of the spiritual life, attained through sustained effort and permanently possessed once attained: repentance, abstinence, renunciation, poverty, patience, trust in God, and acceptance. These are not mere moral accomplishments but ontological transformations, corresponding to progressive levels of consciousness in which the relationship between the self and the divine is fundamentally reorganised at each step. The ahwal — "states" — are the transient graces bestowed by God upon the soul as it traverses the stations: intimacy, awe, proximity, love, longing, and more. Unlike the stations, which are stable acquisitions of the soul's own work, the states are pure gifts that come and go according to the divine will. Together, the stations and states constitute a comprehensive psychology of spiritual ascent, charted with extraordinary precision by masters from Al-Sarraj to Al-Qushayri and beyond, and culminating in the states of fana (annihilation in God) and baqa (subsistence through God) that represent the final stages of the path's consummation.

The Merkabah mystics of late antiquity and the early medieval period — whose visionary texts describe the ascent of the soul through seven heavenly palaces (Hekhalot) to the divine Throne (Merkabah) — offer one of the most dramatic and vivid accounts of spiritual ascension in the Jewish tradition. The Hekhalot ("palaces") are not merely metaphors but are described with extraordinary concreteness: each palace has specific guardians who must be passed with specific passwords and specific seals; each level of the ascent requires specific qualifications and generates specific dangers for the unprepared traveller. The successful ascent culminates in a vision of the divine Glory (Kavod) enthroned on the Merkabah — an experience of overwhelming beauty and terror that permanently transforms the visionary. The Merkabah tradition is significant not merely as an ancient curiosity but as one of the earliest systematic accounts of guided interior ascent in the Western tradition, and it fed directly into the Kabbalistic tradition's account of the soul's relationship to the divine through the sefirot.

The Hermetic tradition describes the soul's post-mortem — and potentially ante-mortem — ascent through the seven planetary spheres as a process of progressive divestiture and illumination. As the soul ascends through the sphere of the Moon, it sheds the capacity for growth and diminishment; through Mercury, the capacity for cunning and deception; through Venus, the capacity for desire; through the Sun, the arrogant rule of the powerful; through Mars, the impulse toward hasty and reckless action; through Jupiter, the impulse toward acquisitiveness; and through Saturn, the impulse toward falsehood and wickedness. What remains, having shed all these planetary garments, is the soul in its pure divine nature — radically simplified, stripped of all the accretions that the descent through the spheres imposed upon it, ready to enter the eighth sphere of the fixed stars and ultimately the divine source beyond. This Hermetic account of ascension as a process of shedding and simplification is structurally parallel to the Sufi path of progressive renunciation and to the Neoplatonic account of the soul's return through the reversal of its descent from the One.

Buddhist nirvana — literally the "extinguishing" of the fires of craving, aversion, and delusion — represents perhaps the most radical formulation of illumination in any of the world's traditions, because it insists on the complete cessation of the conditions that generate the ordinary experience of a separate self as the necessary condition for liberation. Nirvana is not an ascent to a higher dimension of the same reality experienced in ordinary consciousness but the complete release from the entire structure of conditioned existence (samsara). The Mahayana tradition, in developing the bodhisattva ideal, complicates and enriches this account: the bodhisattva, having reached the threshold of final nirvana, voluntarily postpones their own complete liberation in order to work for the liberation of all sentient beings, returning again and again to the conditioned world out of compassion rather than compulsion. This introduces into the Buddhist account of illumination a relational and ethical dimension — a recognition that the final stage of the path is not a retreat into solitary transcendence but a return to the world transformed, the illumined consciousness becoming a vehicle of compassionate action for the benefit of all.

The Tantric traditions of both Hindu and Buddhist provenance describe the ascent of consciousness through the chakra system — the seven energy centres aligned along the spine — as a physiology of illumination. The kundalini, the dormant spiritual energy coiled at the base of the spine like a sleeping serpent, is awakened through practice and rises through each chakra in turn — Muladhara (root), Svadhisthana (sacral), Manipura (solar plexus), Anahata (heart), Vishuddha (throat), Ajna (third eye), and finally Sahasrara (crown) — each rising associated with specific experiences, powers (siddhis), and states of consciousness. The arrival of the kundalini at the crown centre is described as the experience of supreme union (samadhi or mahamudra): the individual consciousness dissolving into or recognising its identity with the universal consciousness. The Tantric account is distinctive in its insistence that this ascent is not merely a matter of consciousness but involves the physical body in a genuine way: the body is not merely the container for a spiritual process happening elsewhere, but the very vehicle and instrument through which the supreme ascension occurs.

The danger that Jung identified as "inflation" — the unconscious identification of the ego with archetypal or divine contents that properly belong to the Self rather than to the individual ego — is perhaps the most common and most seductive form of spiritual error on the path toward illumination. The practitioner who has had a genuine experience of expanded consciousness — a genuine glimpse of the illumined awareness that is the goal of the path — can mistake the glimpse for the completion, and begin to relate to themselves as though the illumination is now a permanent and stable possession rather than an intimation of what is still to be achieved. This inflation produces a characteristic syndrome: grandiosity, decreased capacity for genuine relationship, a subtle rigidity in the face of challenges to one's own spiritual self-image, and an inability to recognise the unlived and unintegrated dimensions of one's own psyche. The authentic traditions guard against this in various ways — through the requirement of a teacher qualified to recognise and correct inflation, through the insistence that genuine illumination must be tested by its fruits in ordinary life, and through the frank acknowledgement that the path continues far beyond the first glimpse of the goal.

Key Concepts

☦️

Theosis

Orthodox Christian doctrine of deification: the soul's genuine participation in the divine energies (not the divine essence) through sustained practice, sacramental life, and the grace of the Holy Spirit. "God became human that humans might become God" — Athanasius of Alexandria.

🌊

Fana/Baqa

Sufi states of annihilation in God (fana) and subsistence through God (baqa). Fana is the dissolution of the ego in the divine presence; baqa is the return to ordinary life as a transformed instrument of the divine will — illumination embodied and expressed in the world.

🏛️

Hekhalot

The heavenly palaces of Merkabah mysticism through which the Jewish visionary ascends to the divine Throne. Each palace requires specific passwords, seals, and preparations; the ascent culminates in the vision of the divine Kavod (Glory) that permanently transforms the successful practitioner.

🐍

Kundalini

The serpent power that, when awakened through Tantric practice, ascends the spine through the seven chakras toward the crown, where its arrival is experienced as the union of individual and cosmic consciousness. The Tantric tradition insists that this ascent involves the physical body as genuine instrument and vehicle.

☸️

Nirvana

Buddhist liberation from the cycle of conditioned existence — the extinguishing of craving, aversion, and delusion. The Mahayana bodhisattva ideal adds a compassionate dimension: the illumined one returns to the conditioned world to work for the liberation of all beings before accepting final nirvana.

🌟

Lux Mentis

The "light of the mind" in Neoplatonism and medieval mysticism — the illumined intellect in direct contact with the divine Intellect. Plotinus's description of the nous in its highest state, Albert the Great's concept of the intellectus adeptus, and the medieval mystical concept of the "scintilla animae" (spark of the soul) are all expressions of this illumined condition.

Key Scholars

Plotinus (c. 204–270 CE)

Egyptian-Roman philosopher and author of the Enneads — the most rigorous philosophical account of mystical ascent in the Western tradition. Plotinus's personal accounts of union with the One, and his systematic philosophical account of how the soul ascends through Intellect to touch the One, remain the foundational text of Western philosophical mysticism.

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500 CE)

Anonymous Syrian Christian Neoplatonist and author of The Mystical Theology and The Divine Names. Pseudo-Dionysius's apophatic (negative) theology — the insistence that the divine transcends all positive predication and is approached through the progressive negation of all names and concepts — became the template for Christian mystical ascent throughout the medieval period.

Al-Ghazali (1058–1111)

Persian Sufi philosopher and theologian, author of The Revival of the Religious Sciences and The Niche of Lights. Al-Ghazali's synthesis of Sufi illuminationism with orthodox Islamic theology — his account of how the divine light descends through levels of being to illuminate the prepared heart — is one of the most influential accounts of spiritual illumination in the Islamic tradition.

Idries Shah

Afghan-British author of The Sufis (1964) and numerous subsequent works on Sufi thought and practice. Shah's accessible yet sophisticated account of Sufi illuminationism — and his argument that the Sufi path of illumination underlies many Western esoteric and cultural traditions — brought the concept of spiritual ascension to a wide twentieth-century audience.
