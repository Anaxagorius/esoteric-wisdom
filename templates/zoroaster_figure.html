Zoroaster

Zoroaster — known in the Avestan language as Zarathustra — was the ancient Iranian prophet and poet-sage who founded Zoroastrianism, one of the world's oldest monotheistic religions. His vision of a cosmos ordered by the eternal conflict between Ahura Mazda (Wise Lord) and Angra Mainyu (Destructive Spirit) shaped not only Persian religion but profoundly influenced Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and the Western esoteric tradition.

Life & Historical Context

The dating of Zoroaster remains one of the most contested questions in the history of religion. Ancient Greek sources placed him extraordinarily early — 5,000 or even 6,000 years before the Trojan War — reflecting the Greek perception of him as a figure of primordial antiquity and magical power. Modern scholars have proposed dates ranging from approximately 1500–1000 BCE (based on the archaic language of the Gathas, Zoroaster's own hymns) to the 7th–6th century BCE, roughly contemporary with the Hebrew prophets and the Upanishadic sages of India.

The Gathas — seventeen hymns preserved in the sacred text of the Avesta — are our most direct source for Zoroaster's own thought and experience. In these intensely personal poems, composed in an archaic dialect of Old Avestan, Zoroaster describes his prophetic call, his struggles against opponents who worshipped the daēvas (demonised deities), and his vision of a cosmic moral order governed by Ahura Mazda. The hymns reveal a man of extraordinary spiritual vision and also of considerable personal suffering: he complains of persecution, of exile, of the difficulty of finding patrons willing to support his reform of the traditional Iranian religion.

Zoroaster's eventual patron was King Vishtaspa (possibly the father of Darius the Great, though this identification is disputed), whose conversion to the new faith gave Zoroastrianism the royal support it needed to spread. The prophet himself was said to have been martyred during the sack of the sacred fire-temple at Balkh — a death that reinforced the paradigm of the prophet as a figure who suffers for the truth he proclaims.

Cosmic Dualism & Sacred Fire

The cardinal doctrine of Zoroastrianism is ethical and cosmic dualism: the universe is the battleground of two primal spirits — Spenta Mainyu, the Holy Spirit (later identified with Ahura Mazda himself) and Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), the Destructive Spirit. These two spirits chose, at the beginning of time, between truth and falsehood, life and death, light and darkness. Every human being is called to choose likewise, and the collective choices of humanity determine the outcome of the cosmic conflict, which will culminate in the Frashokereti — the final renovation of the world, in which evil is annihilated and all of creation returns to its original purity.

Fire held supreme sacred significance in Zoroastrian worship as the visible emblem of Ahura Mazda's divine light and truth (asha). The great fire temples of ancient Iran — Adur Gushnasp, Adur Farnbag, and Adur Burzen-Mihr — were regarded as sacred centres of cosmic energy, and the maintenance of the sacred flame was a priestly duty of the highest importance. The Magi, the Zoroastrian priestly caste of the Medes and Persians, became famous throughout the ancient world as astrologers, diviners, and fire-priests — and it is from this caste that the word "magic" ultimately derives.

Influence on Western Esotericism

In the Graeco-Roman world, Zoroaster was regarded with immense reverence as the archetypal magus — a figure of supreme wisdom who had mastered the secrets of astronomy, astrology, natural magic, and the hidden powers of the cosmos. He was credited with having invented magic and astrology, and his name was attached to numerous spurious texts on these subjects. The Chaldaean Oracles — a second-century CE text of enormous influence in later Neoplatonism and Hermetism — was attributed to a "Julianus the Theurgist" but claimed to represent the wisdom of Zoroaster and the Chaldaean magi.

Zoroastrian eschatology — its doctrines of the Last Judgement, the individual soul's fate after death (the crossing of the Chinvat Bridge, where the soul encounters its own deeds made flesh), the resurrection of the body, and the final renovation of the cosmos — profoundly shaped Jewish apocalypticism during the Persian period (6th–4th centuries BCE), and through Judaism influenced Christianity and Islam. Scholars have traced striking parallels between Zoroastrian doctrines and the Dead Sea Scrolls' dualism of the Sons of Light and Sons of Darkness.

Nietzsche's Also Sprach Zarathustra (1883–85) gave Zoroaster a second life in Western philosophical culture, using the prophet as a mouthpiece for the proclamation of the Superman and the transvaluation of all values — though Nietzsche's Zarathustra is a deliberate inversion of the historical prophet's ethical dualism. Nonetheless, this literary resurrection ensured that the name Zarathustra would resonate in Western culture far beyond its specifically religious context.

Legacy

Zoroastrianism survives today primarily among the Parsis of India (descendants of Iranian Zoroastrians who fled the Islamic conquest of Persia in the 7th–10th centuries CE) and the remaining Zoroastrians of Iran. Despite its relatively small numbers — perhaps 100,000–200,000 adherents worldwide — the tradition maintains its ancient liturgical language, its sacred fire temples, and its distinctive moral framework of good thoughts, good words, and good deeds (humata, hūkhta, hvarshta).

Zoroaster's legacy in the history of religion is disproportionate to the current size of his tradition. His radical monotheism, his ethical dualism, his doctrine of free moral choice as the engine of cosmic history, and his vision of a universal eschatological renovation have shaped the three great Abrahamic faiths and through them the moral consciousness of Western civilisation. In the Western esoteric tradition, he continues to represent the archetype of the prophet-mage: the figure who receives divine revelation and translates it into a practical teaching for the transformation of the world.
