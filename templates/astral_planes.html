Astral Planes & Subtle Bodies

The invisible dimensions of existence — the layered architecture of reality beyond the physical, and the subtle vehicles through which consciousness travels.

The Doctrine of Subtle Bodies

One of the most consistent convictions across the great esoteric traditions of the world is that the human being is not simply a physical body animated by a single, simple soul, but a multilayered composite being — a series of increasingly subtle bodies or vehicles of consciousness, interpenetrating and surrounding the dense physical form like the skins of an onion or the concentric circles of a target. This doctrine, independently developed in ancient Egypt, India, Greece, Tibet, and China, points towards a universal feature of the inner life that transcends any particular cultural expression.

In ancient Egypt, the human being was understood to consist of multiple components. The khat (physical body) was animated by the ka (vital double or etheric body) — depicted as a person’s exact double — and the ba (often translated as “soul” — depicted as a human-headed bird that could leave the body in sleep and after death) was the aspect of the person most associated with consciousness and identity. The akh was the perfected, luminous spiritual being that the deceased person could become through proper preparation and ritual — a genuinely divine, imperishable mode of being beyond the limitations of the physical and even the subtle.

In ancient Greek philosophy, the concept of pneuma (πνεῦμα — breath, spirit, or subtle matter) served as the vehicle of life and consciousness intermediate between the gross physical body and the rational soul. Aristotle used pneuma to explain the transmission of vital force in reproduction and the vehicle of the intellectual soul’s activities. The Stoics developed a thoroughgoing pneuma-philosophy in which the entire cosmos was permeated by a rational, divine pneuma that constituted the World Soul and animated every living thing. The Neoplatonists spoke of the ochema (vehicle or chariot) of the soul — a subtle, luminous body in which the soul descends into incarnation and ascends after death.

In the Indian tradition, the doctrine of subtle bodies receives its most systematic development in the Tantric and Vedantic concept of the kosha (sheath or envelope) system and the prana-body or pranamaya kosha. The Taittiriya Upanishad (c. 7th–5th century BCE) describes five sheaths (koshas) that envelop and conceal the Atman (the true Self): the food-body (annamaya), the vital-force body (pranamaya), the mental body (manomaya), the intellect body (vijnanamaya), and the bliss body (anandamaya). These sheaths become progressively more subtle and closer to the pure consciousness of the Atman as one proceeds inward.

The Tibetan Buddhist tradition develops a sophisticated subtle-body doctrine in its Tantric and Dzogchen teachings. The “vajra body” consists of subtle channels (rtsa or nadi), vital winds or energies (rlung or prana), and essential drops (thig le or bindu). The central channel (avadhuti) runs from the crown of the head to the base of the spine, with subsidiary channels branching from it at the major chakra-points. The practices of the Highest Yoga Tantra — tummo (inner heat), the yoga of the dream state, and the yoga of the clear light of death — all work directly with this subtle-body system.

The Chinese tradition speaks of the jing (essence), qi (vital force or breath), and shen (spirit) as the three treasures of the human being, corresponding to the physical, the vital, and the spiritual dimensions. Taoist inner alchemy (nei dan) is explicitly concerned with the purification and refinement of these three substances, circulating them through the body’s subtle channels and energy centres (dantian) to achieve immortality and spiritual realisation.

The Astral Plane: What Is It?

The term “astral plane” entered Western esoteric vocabulary primarily through Theosophical writings of the late 19th century, though its conceptual roots are much older. In Theosophical and subsequent New Age usage, the astral plane is the second of the seven cosmic planes — the plane immediately “above” or “inner” to the physical, characterised by the quality of desire, emotion, and imagination. It is sometimes called the Kamaloka (Desire World) in Theosophical terminology.

The astral plane is understood as a realm of extraordinary vividness and fluidity: thought and desire directly shape the astral environment, so that the beings who inhabit the astral (including human beings in the astral body during sleep, after death, or during out-of-body experiences) encounter a reality that is at once more vivid and more malleable than physical reality. The astral light — a concept central to Eliphas Lévi’s occultism — is the great reflective medium of the astral plane, in which all thoughts, images, and desires are mirrored and preserved. It corresponds in some respects to the collective unconscious and to the “imagination” in the Hermetic sense: not mere fantasy but the faculty of image-making through which non-physical realities become accessible to consciousness.

The astral plane is densely populated by a variety of entities in esoteric literature: the shells (the discarded astral bodies of deceased humans, gradually disintegrating), elementals (thought-forms created by human desire and imagination, given temporary autonomous life by the force of their creation), nature spirits (elemental beings associated with the four classical elements), and various classes of astral entities with their own evolutionary trajectories. The practitioner navigating the astral plane must develop discrimination to distinguish between authentic spiritual encounters and the reflections of their own unconscious projections.

The Theosophical Seven-Plane System

Adi & Anupadaka

1st & 2nd Planes

The highest and most transcendent planes — the Adi (first or divine) plane and the Anupadaka (parentless or monadic) plane. The domain of the Absolute and of the highest spiritual monads. Beyond the reach of ordinary human consciousness or description.

Atmic Plane

3rd Plane

The plane of the Atma — the pure spiritual will and universal divine Self. The locus of Nirvana in the Theosophical system. The Atman is the divine spark at the core of every human being, identical with the universal Brahman. Accessible through the deepest meditation and spiritual realisation.

Buddhic Plane

4th Plane

The plane of the Buddhi — pure spiritual intuition, universal compassion, and the direct perception of unity. The Buddha-nature in Mahayana terms. The Buddhic plane is the realm of causal consciousness, where individual selves are recognised as expressions of a single universal life. The vehicle here is the causal body.

Mental Plane

5th Plane

The plane of the mind — divided into the higher (abstract) and lower (concrete) mental sub-planes. The higher mental is the realm of archetypal ideas and abstract thought; the lower mental is the realm of the concrete, reasoning mind. The mental body is the vehicle of thought and the seat of the individual ego in its most refined form.

Astral Plane

6th Plane

The plane of desire, emotion, and the imagination — the realm through which the soul passes after physical death before ascending to the mental plane. The astral body is the vehicle of emotional experience. Vivid, fluid, and responsive to thought and desire. The realm of dreams, out-of-body experiences, and the intermediate state after death.

Etheric & Physical

7th Plane

The physical plane — divided into the dense physical (solid, liquid, gaseous matter) and the etheric (the four higher sub-planes of physical matter, the vital energy field interpenetrating the dense body). The etheric body or “health aura” conducts prana into the physical body and is the foundation of vitality and health. Disease often appears in the etheric before manifesting physically.

Subtle Body Systems in Comparison

The structural parallels between the subtle body systems of different traditions are striking enough to suggest that they are independently mapping the same underlying reality. The Hindu kosha system of five sheaths (food body, vital body, mental body, wisdom body, bliss body) corresponds broadly to the Theosophical layers of physical, etheric, astral/emotional, mental, and causal/buddhic. The Tibetan subtle-body system of channels, winds, and drops corresponds to the Hindu nadi, prana, and bindu system. The Western alchemical and Hermetic layers of physical, vital (etheric), astral, mental, and spiritual bodies correspond to all of the above.

These correspondences are not identity — each tradition has its own emphases, terminologies, and practical methods — but they suggest a common recognition of the multi-layered structure of consciousness and embodiment. The human being is not simply a physical body but a series of increasingly subtle, increasingly luminous vehicles, each interpenetrating the others, each the vehicle of a different mode of consciousness, and each subject to its own type of development and refinement through appropriate spiritual practice.

The practical implications of the subtle-body doctrine are enormous: illness may originate in the subtle bodies before manifesting in the physical; spiritual development involves the progressive purification and refinement of the subtle vehicles; death involves the sequential dissolution of the subtle bodies from the grossest to the most refined, each dissolution constituting a passage through a different level of post-mortem experience; and the path of liberation involves the transcendence of identification with each successive vehicle until only the pure, formless Atman or buddha-nature remains.

Astral Projection & Conscious Exploration

The Mechanics of Astral Travel

In esoteric literature, astral projection — the deliberate separation of the astral body from the physical during life — is understood to occur naturally every night during sleep (when the astral and mental bodies partially withdraw from the physical), during near-death experiences, and during the deeper states of meditation and trance. The silver cord — a luminous thread connecting the astral body to the physical — is a consistent feature of many accounts, both spontaneous and deliberate. It is understood to maintain the connection between the bodies and to permit the return of the astral traveller to the physical body.

Monroe Institute & Gateway Process

Robert Monroe’s pioneering research into out-of-body experiences, documented in his trilogy (Journeys Out of the Body, 1971; Far Journeys, 1985; Ultimate Journey, 1994), brought the phenomenon of astral travel into secular, research-based discourse. The Monroe Institute’s Gateway Programme uses Hemi-Sync audio technology to facilitate altered states of consciousness conducive to out-of-body experience. The CIA’s declassified assessment of the Gateway Process acknowledged the experiential reality of what Monroe described. See the dedicated Gateway Process page for further detail.

Cosmological Significance

The planes of being are not merely levels of personal psychology but dimensions of the objective cosmos. The Theosophical and Hermetic traditions insist that each plane is inhabited by its own orders of beings: the physical plane by humanity and the other visible creatures; the etheric by nature spirits and the forces of organic life; the astral by desire-elementals, shells, and astral entities; the mental by beings of pure thought; the buddhic and atmic by advanced spiritual intelligences. This is not merely a human-centred system: it is a cosmology of the entire invisible universe.

The planes are also the stages of the soul’s journey. At death, consciousness withdraws from the physical through the etheric (the process of dying), then through the astral (the Kamaloka or purgatorial state in which the soul purifies its emotional nature), then into the mental and eventually the higher planes, where the spiritual essence distils the wisdom of the life just completed before descending again into incarnation. The entire cosmos is thus a school, a vehicle for the soul’s progressive development through multiple lives and multiple planes of experience, drawing ever closer to the divine source from which it originally proceeded.
