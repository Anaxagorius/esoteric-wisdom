Japanese Religion

From the indigenous kami-worship of Shinto and the many schools of Japanese Buddhism to the syncretic fusion of Shinbutsu-shūgō, the esoteric divination of Onmyōdō, and vibrant new religious movements — the sacred landscape of Japan in full.

🏯 Native Japanese Religion: Shinto

Shinto (kami no michi, "Way of the Kami") is Japan's indigenous religion. It centres on kami — spirits, forces, and presences inhabiting nature, ancestors, and sacred sites. Shinto is not a creed or theology but a living matrix of ritual, seasonal festivals, shrine practice, and cultural identity.

⛩️ Jinja Shinto (Shrine Shinto)

The most common and normative form — centred on shrine (jinja) veneration, matsuri (festivals), and priestly lineages. Overseen by the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honchō) since 1946.

🏛️ State Shinto (Kokka Shinto)

The nationalised state religion of imperial Japan from the Meiji Restoration (1868) to the end of World War II (1945). Emperor veneration, nationalist myth, and shrine patronage were institutionalised by the state before Allied-era disestablishment.

🏘️ Sect Shinto (Kyōha Shinto)

Thirteen officially recognised sects that emerged in the 19th century with distinct founders, doctrines, or practices — including Tenrikyō (before independence), Kurozumikyō, and Konkokyō.

🌾 Folk Shinto (Minkan Shinto)

Local and village religious practices not attached to any organised institution — household kami-dana altars, roadside stone deities, agricultural rites, and communal festivals woven into daily rural life.

🧘 Buddhism in Japan

Buddhism arrived in Japan via Korea and China in the 6th century CE and developed into a remarkable array of distinct schools across successive historical periods, each adapting the Dharma to Japanese culture, aristocratic patronage, and popular needs.

📜 Nara Schools (Early Buddhism, 710–794 CE)

Historically foundational schools patronised by the imperial court; today less active as independent communities but important for intellectual heritage.

Kegon — Based on the Avatamsaka Sutra; Tōdai-ji temple in Nara is its centre.
Ritsu — Emphasis on monastic discipline and Vinaya precepts.
Hossō — Yogācāra school; mind-only philosophy; Kōfuku-ji temple.
Sanron — Mādhyamaka emptiness philosophy brought from China.
Jōjitsu — Early Abhidharma analysis of dharmas; absorbed into Sanron.
Kusha — Abhidharma scholasticism; absorbed into Hossō.

🏔️ Heian Period Schools (794–1185 CE)

Tendai (Tiantai)

Founded by Saichō (Dengyō Daishi) on Mount Hiei near Kyoto. A comprehensive school teaching the Lotus Sutra, esoteric practices, and meditation. Served as a crucible from which many later Japanese Buddhist movements arose.

Shingon (True Word)

Japanese Esoteric Buddhism founded by Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) at Mount Kōya. Practices the Three Mysteries of body (mudra), speech (mantra), and mind (mandala visualisation) to attain Buddhahood in this very body (sokushin jōbutsu).

🌅 Pure Land Buddhism

Among the most widely practised forms of Buddhism in Japan, centred on faith in Amida Buddha and rebirth in the Western Pure Land.

Jōdo-shū (Pure Land School)

Founded by Hōnen (1133–1212). Salvation through single-minded recitation of the nembutsu: Namu Amida Butsu.

Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land)

Founded by Shinran (1173–1263), a disciple of Hōnen. The largest Buddhist denomination in Japan. Emphasises absolute reliance on Amida's tariki (other-power) rather than self-power practice.

🪷 Zen Buddhism

Transmission of awakening through direct experience, meditation, teacher-student dialogue, and koān practice rather than doctrinal study alone.

Rinzai Zen

Brought to Japan by Eisai (1141–1215). Intensive koān practice, sudden awakening (satori), and strong ties to samurai culture and the arts.

Sōtō Zen

Introduced by Dōgen (1200–1253). Emphasises shikantaza — "just sitting" — and the identity of practice and enlightenment. Largest Zen school in Japan.

Ōbaku Zen

Introduced in the 17th century by the Chinese master Yinyuan Longqi. Blends Zen meditation with nembutsu recitation and Ming-dynasty Chinese ritual aesthetics.

📖 Nichiren Buddhism

Founded by the monk Nichiren (1222–1282), who taught that the Lotus Sutra is the supreme teaching of the Buddha and that its chanting — Nam-myōhō-renge-kyō — is the universal path to enlightenment.

Nichiren-shū — The original mainstream Nichiren denomination centred at Mount Minobu.
Nichiren Shōshū — A stricter branch based at Taiseki-ji, Mt Fuji; formerly the lay-organisation base for Soka Gakkai.
Soka Gakkai — A major modern lay movement derived from Nichiren Shōshū; now independent, globally active, and politically engaged through its party Kōmeitō.

🌓 Syncretic Tradition: Shinbutsu-shūgō

Shinbutsu-shūgō (神仏習合, "blending of kami and Buddhas") describes the centuries-long fusion of Shinto and Buddhism that dominated Japanese religious life from the Nara period until the Meiji-era forced separation (shinbutsu bunri) of 1868. Within this synthesis, kami were interpreted as local manifestations of Buddhas and Bodhisattvas (honji suijaku), and Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines shared precincts and priests. Despite official separation, syncretic practices persist in everyday Japanese religious life today.

Shugendō

A powerful manifestation of Shinbutsu-shūgō — mountain asceticism combining Shinto animism, esoteric Shingon and Tendai Buddhism, and Taoist elements. Practitioners (yamabushi) undergo rigorous mountain retreats on sacred peaks such as Kinpusen, Haguro, and Ōmine.

🌿 Folk Religion

Japanese folk religion (minkan shinkō) operates beneath and alongside institutional Shinto and Buddhism, expressing itself in everyday domestic and communal ritual.

  • Ancestor worship (sosen sūhai): Regular offerings and memorial rites at the household butsudan (Buddhist altar) keep the living in relationship with the spirits of the dead.
  • Kami-dana: The household Shinto shelf holding talismans, sacred ropes (shimenawa), and offerings to house and tutelary kami.
  • Yōkai and spirit beliefs: A rich taxonomy of supernatural beings — ghosts (yūrei), mischievous spirits (yōkai), and fox spirits (kitsune) — embedded in folklore and protective ritual.
  • Seasonal festivals (matsuri): Community celebrations tied to agricultural cycles, shrine calendars, and propitiation of local deities.
  • O-mamori and pilgrimage: Amulets from shrines and temples, and pilgrimage circuits such as the 88-temple Shikoku circuit (o-henro), remain central to lay practice.

🌏 Confucianism

Confucianism entered Japan via China and Korea and, though not a religion in the Western sense, profoundly shaped Japanese ethics, social hierarchy, family structure, loyalty (chū), filial piety (kō), and bureaucratic governance. Neo-Confucianism was highly influential in Edo-period intellectual life.

🧠 Taoism (Daoism)

Taoist cosmology and practices influenced Japanese religion through cosmology, astrology, calendrical reckoning, and especially the yin-yang (onmyō) system. Taoist elements were absorbed into the Onmyōdō practice, Shinto ritual purity concepts, and esoteric Buddhism.

🔮 Onmyōdō — The Way of Yin and Yang

Onmyōdō (陰陽道) is a distinctly Japanese esoteric system that synthesises Taoist cosmology, Buddhist metaphysics, and Shinto ritual into a unified practice of divination, geomancy, exorcism, and ritual protection. It flourished as an official court discipline from the Nara through Heian periods under the Onmyōryō bureau, with the Abe and Kamo clans holding hereditary expertise.

Foundations

  • Yin-yang (onmyō) cosmology
  • Five phases / Wu Xing (earth, water, fire, metal, wood)
  • Heavenly stems and earthly branches (calendar system)

Practices

  • Divination (uranai) for auspicious timing
  • Exorcism and spirit appeasement (chinkon)
  • Astrology and star-deity worship
  • Direction taboos (katatagae)

Key Figures

  • Abe no Seimei (921–1005) — the most celebrated Onmyōji, celebrated in legend and popular culture
  • The Abe and Kamo hereditary lineages

🌟 New Religious Movements (Shinshūkyō)

Japan produced an extraordinary wave of new religions from the 19th century onwards, often drawing on Shinto, Buddhist, Confucian, or shamanic elements while introducing new revelation, healing practices, or millenarian expectation.

Early New Religions (19th century)

Tenrikyō

Founded 1838 by Nakayama Miki after a series of divine possessions. Centres on Jiba (the sacred site in Tenri City) and the joyous life (yōkigurashi) through ritual practice and charity.

Konkokyō

Founded 1859 by Kawate Bunjirō. A mediating religion seeking harmony between humans and Tenchi Kane no Kami (Parent God of Universe), emphasising prayer and spiritual mediation.

Kurozumikyō

Founded 1814 by Kurozumi Munetada, making it among the earliest. Worships Amaterasu as the one great deity and teaches unity with the divine through joyful faith.

Buddhist-Derived Movements

Soka Gakkai

Founded 1930 by Makiguchi Tsunesaburō as an educational lay movement aligned with Nichiren Shōshū. Now independent and globally active with millions of members; politically engaged through the Kōmeitō party.

Risshō Kōsei Kai

Founded 1938 by Niwano Nikkyō and Naganuma Myōkō. A lay Nichiren Buddhist movement emphasising the Lotus Sutra, mutual counselling (hōza), and interfaith engagement.

Other New Religions

Seicho-no-Ie

Founded 1930 by Taniguchi Masaharu. Teaches that reality is fundamentally spiritual and harmonious; draws on Shinto, Buddhist, Christian, and metaphysical healing traditions.

Perfect Liberty Kyodan (PL Kyodan)

Founded 1946 by Miki Tokuharu and Miki Tokuchika. Centres on the principle that "life is art" — all human activity is self-expression and a form of worship.

⚠️ Smaller and Controversial New Religions

Japan's religious landscape also includes movements that attracted significant controversy, state scrutiny, or public alarm, particularly those that combined apocalyptic doctrine with unorthodox or harmful practices.

Aum Shinrikyō (now Aleph)

Founded 1984 by Asahara Shōkō. Drew eclectically on Hindu tantra, Tibetan Buddhism, and Christian apocalypticism. Perpetrated the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack, killing 13 and injuring thousands. Classified as a dangerous organisation in Japan and internationally. Continues under the name Aleph and splinter group Hikari no Wa, both under ongoing state surveillance.

Numerous smaller sects, charismatic movements, and modern spiritual groups — drawing variously on New Age currents, UFO religion, and folk healing — continue to emerge within Japan's highly pluralistic religious environment.
