Sufism

The mystical heart of Islam — the Way of Love, the annihilation of self in the Divine Beloved, and the ecstatic poetry that has carried the deepest waters of the tradition across a thousand years and a dozen languages.

Introduction

Sufism — known in Arabic as Tasawwuf — is the inner, mystical dimension of Islam. It emerged in the early centuries of Islamic history as a movement of intense piety and inward devotion, its name most probably derived from suf (wool), the rough woollen garments worn by early Muslim ascetics as a sign of their renunciation of worldly comforts. From these austere beginnings grew one of the world's great mystical traditions — a tradition of extraordinary richness, intellectual depth, and aesthetic beauty, producing some of the finest poetry, music, and philosophy the world has known. Sufism understands the outer forms of Islamic practice — prayer, fasting, pilgrimage — not as ends in themselves but as the beginning of a much deeper inward journey toward direct experience of the Divine.

The Sufi path is structured around the concept of tariqa (way or path) — a lineage of transmission from master to student that traces itself back, through an unbroken chain of teachers (silsila), to the Prophet Muhammad and, through him, to the Divine. The tariqa provides the framework: a sheikh (spiritual master), a khanqah or tekke (gathering place), a practice of dhikr (remembrance), a curriculum of spiritual states and stations, and a community of fellow travellers. Within this framework, the student undergoes a systematic transformation of the self — from the unregenerate nafs (ego-self) dominated by desire and illusion, through progressively purified states, toward the ultimate goal of fana: the annihilation of the small self in the infinite ocean of God.

The division between the sober and intoxicated schools of Sufism reflects a fundamental difference of temperament and theology. The sober school, associated with Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) and Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), emphasises rigorous self-discipline, careful adherence to Islamic law, and measured language about the experience of union — understanding fana not as literal identity with God but as a temporary state of consciousness in which the individual self is overwhelmed by the Divine presence. The intoxicated school, associated with Bayazid Bistami (804–874) and most dramatically with Mansur al-Hallaj, who famously declared ana'l-Haqq ("I am the Truth/God") and was executed for it in 922, speaks with scandalous directness about the dissolution of every boundary between lover and Beloved. Between these poles lies the full spectrum of Sufi expression.

The greatest theoretical systematiser of Sufi metaphysics was Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), the Andalusian mystic whose concept of wahdat al-wujud (the Unity of Being) holds that there is ultimately only one Reality — God — of which the entire cosmos is the self-manifestation. Every grain of sand, every human soul, every celestial being is a Name of God made temporarily visible. Ibn Arabi's extraordinary output — over 350 works, including the monumental Futuhat al-Makkiyya (The Meccan Revelations) — constitutes the most sophisticated metaphysical system produced by any mystical tradition, and has influenced every subsequent school of Sufi thought.

Core Concepts

💨

Fana (Annihilation)

The dissolution of the individual ego-self in the ocean of the Divine — the central mystical goal of the Sufi path. Fana does not mean physical death or the annihilation of consciousness, but the death of the illusion of separate selfhood. In fana, the mystic ceases to experience themselves as an independent entity distinct from God; the lover is overwhelmed by the Beloved and realises that the Beloved was always the only reality.

🌿

Baqa (Subsistence)

The complement of fana — "subsistence" or "abiding" in God after the experience of annihilation. Where fana is the dissolution, baqa is the reconstitution: the mystic returns to the world, but as a vessel of the Divine rather than a self-enclosed ego. In baqa, the realised Sufi lives and acts in the world but is no longer identified with their actions; the Divine acts through them as an instrument. This is the state of the great Sufi teachers and saints.

📿

Dhikr (Remembrance)

The central practice of Sufism — the rhythmic repetition of sacred Names and phrases, performed individually or collectively, silently or aloud, with breath and movement, as the primary means of purifying the heart and attuning consciousness to the Divine presence. The dhikr may be as simple as the repetition of "Allah" or as elaborate as a full ceremony with music and movement. At its core, dhikr enacts the Quranic injunction: "Remember God, and your hearts will find rest."

🎵

Sama (Sacred Listening)

The practice of listening to sacred music and poetry as a spiritual discipline — most famously associated with the Mevlevi Order (the "Whirling Dervishes") of Rumi's lineage. Sama is understood as a means of opening the heart to the Divine; the beautiful melody carries the soul beyond the ordinary mind. Whether the ecstatic whirling of the Mevlevi or the concentrated listening of the Chishti qawwali tradition, sama uses beauty as a vehicle of transcendence.

🔗

Silsila (Spiritual Lineage)

The unbroken chain of transmission from sheikh to student that authenticates a Sufi order and connects it to its founding master and, ultimately, to the Prophet. The silsila is not merely historical documentation; it is understood as the living channel through which the spiritual power (baraka) of the tradition flows. A genuine Sufi initiation is the formal entry into this chain — the student receives the baraka transmitted through the sheikh's own initiation, and through every link before it.

🗺️

Maqamat (Stations of the Path)

The structured stages of spiritual development through which the Sufi journeys — each maqam (station) representing a stable, earned attainment: repentance, abstinence, renunciation, poverty, patience, trust in God, satisfaction, and ultimately fana. Unlike the hal (spiritual state), which comes and goes as a divine gift, the maqam is a permanent transformation of the self resulting from sustained practice. The classical Sufi manuals map these stations with remarkable precision and psychological sophistication.

The Sufi Orders

The major Sufi orders (turuq) emerged primarily between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries as the tradition became institutionalised around the tombs and teachings of great masters. The Qadiriyya, founded by Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (1077–1166) in Baghdad, became the most geographically widespread of all Sufi orders — from Morocco to Indonesia, from Senegal to Chechnya. The Naqshbandiyya, tracing its origin to Baha ud-Din Naqshband of Bukhara (1318–1389), is distinguished by its "silent dhikr" (performed in the heart rather than aloud) and its emphasis on sobriety, strict Islamic observance, and involvement in public life — it has historically been the most influential order in Central Asia and the Ottoman court. The Chishtiyya, established in South Asia and particularly celebrated for its use of sama and qawwali devotional music, produced the lineage that gave the world the composer and musician Amir Khusrow. The Mevleviyya, the order of Rumi, with its distinctive sama of whirling, became the prestige order of Ottoman Constantinople.

The relationship between Sufism and orthodox Islam has always been complex and sometimes turbulent. The great theologian and jurist Al-Ghazali (1058–1111) performed an extraordinary service for both traditions when he demonstrated, in his masterwork Ihya Ulum al-Din (The Revival of the Religious Sciences), that Sufi inner experience was not merely compatible with Islamic law and theology but was their deepest fulfilment — that the outer practice of the religion only reached its full meaning when it opened into the inner dimension of direct experience. This reconciliation did not end the tensions, but it gave Sufism a theological legitimacy that allowed it to flourish at the heart of Islamic civilisation for centuries. Today, Sufi orders operate on every continent, and the poetry of Rumi has become arguably the best-selling poetry in the English-speaking world — a remarkable testament to the universal resonance of the tradition's core message.

The Poetry of Divine Love

Poetry is the primary medium through which Sufism has communicated its deepest insights, and the tradition has produced some of the most extraordinary literary work in human history. Jalaluddin Rumi (1207–1273), born in what is now Afghanistan and dying in Konya, Turkey, composed the Masnavi — six volumes and over 25,000 verses of spiritual instruction in the form of stories, parables, and mystical reflections — as well as the Divan-e Shams, a vast collection of lyric poems in the voice of ecstatic love. Rumi's poetry emerged from his friendship with the wandering dervish Shams of Tabriz, whose sudden disappearance plunged Rumi into a grief that he transformed into the supreme expression of the soul's longing for reunion with its divine source. The opening lines of the Masnavi — the lament of the reed flute separated from the reed bed — remain one of the most universally recognised mystical metaphors in world literature.

Hafiz of Shiraz (c. 1315–1390) is considered by many Iranians to be the greatest poet of the Persian language — a master of the ghazal form whose apparently contradictory language oscillates between the wine-house and the mosque, the earthly beloved and the Divine. This deliberate ambiguity is not evasion but strategy: Hafiz uses the vocabulary of sensory pleasure — wine, the tavern, the beauty of the beloved — as a transparent veil for the language of mystical intoxication, divine union, and the reckless abandonment of the self-protective ego. Every Persian household traditionally kept two books: the Quran and the Divan of Hafiz. Rabia al-Adawiyya (717–801), the woman mystic of Basra, introduced the language of pure divine love into Islamic spirituality — declaring that she loved God not from fear of Hell nor hope of Paradise, but for God alone. Her prayers and sayings established the template for all subsequent Sufi love-mysticism.

Recommended Reading

📖

Rumi — The Masnavi

The supreme work of Sufi literature — six books of spiritual instruction in verse, rich with stories, parables, Quranic interpretation, and the most direct expressions of mystical experience in any language. Jawid Mojaddedi's Oxford World's Classics translation (ongoing) is the finest modern English rendering; for a more accessible introduction, Coleman Barks's free translations in The Essential Rumi have introduced millions of Western readers to the tradition.

📚

Annemarie Schimmel

Mystical Dimensions of Islam (1975) — the most comprehensive single-volume academic study of Sufism in English, by the German scholar who devoted her life to the tradition. Schimmel covers the full range of Sufi history, practice, poetry, and theology with the combination of scholarly rigour and genuine appreciation that only comes from decades of immersion in a subject. Her book on Rumi, I Am Wind, You Are Fire (1992), is the essential biographical and literary study.

🌿

Henry Corbin

Alone with the Alone: Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn 'Arabi (1958) — the definitive philosophical study of Ibn Arabi's metaphysics, and one of the most profound works of twentieth-century religious philosophy. Corbin's concept of the mundus imaginalis — the imaginal world as a genuine ontological domain between the sensory and the intellectual — grew directly from his engagement with the Sufi tradition and has proven enormously generative for the study of mysticism.

🌙

Ibn Arabi — The Bezels of Wisdom

Fusus al-Hikam (The Bezels of Wisdom, c. 1229) — Ibn Arabi's most concentrated metaphysical work, presenting the esoteric significance of twenty-seven prophets as "bezels" (settings for divine gems) through whom different aspects of divine wisdom are revealed. R.W.J. Austin's translation (Paulist Press, 1980) with commentary remains the most accessible scholarly edition. Demanding but transformative reading for serious students of Islamic mysticism and Perennial Philosophy.
