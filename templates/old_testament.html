Old Testament

The Hebrew scriptures — the sacred covenant record of God and Israel, encompassing creation, law, prophecy, wisdom, and the long drama of divine promise and human response.

What Is the Old Testament?

The Old Testament is the Christian name for the collection of scriptures inherited from the Jewish tradition, known in Judaism as the Tanakh. It comprises 39 books in the Protestant canon (with additional deuterocanonical books in Catholic and Orthodox traditions), spanning poetry, narrative, law, prophecy, and wisdom. Written primarily in Biblical Hebrew with some Aramaic, these texts record the covenantal relationship between God (YHWH) and the people of Israel from creation to the eve of the Second Temple period.

Structure: Torah, Prophets, and Writings

The Hebrew canon is traditionally divided into three parts. The Torah (Pentateuch: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy) records creation, the patriarchal narratives, the Exodus, and the Mosaic law. The Nevi'im (Prophets) includes the historical books (Joshua to Kings) and the prophetic books (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the twelve minor prophets). The Ketuvim (Writings) contains Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, and Chronicles.

Major Themes

The Old Testament is unified by the themes of covenant, creation, fall, redemption, and promised restoration. God creates the world as good, humanity falls through disobedience, and divine initiative continually works toward reconciliation — through Noah, Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets. The Psalms offer the full range of human prayer, from lamentation to praise. The Wisdom literature (Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes) grapples with suffering, meaning, and the fear of the Lord as the beginning of wisdom.

Deuterocanonical and Apocryphal Books

Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions include additional books not found in the Hebrew canon, known as the Deuterocanon or Apocrypha: Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), and Baruch. These texts were part of the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures) and were included in the Latin Vulgate. Protestant Reformers, following the Hebrew canon, treated them as non-canonical, though they have continued to inform Christian theology and spirituality.

Mystical and Esoteric Dimensions

The Old Testament has inspired deep mystical interpretation across centuries. Jewish Kabbalah draws on Genesis for the structure of creation and the nature of the divine. Christian allegorical reading (following Origen, Ambrose, and Augustine) finds layers of spiritual meaning beneath the literal sense. The prophetic visions of Ezekiel's chariot (Merkabah) and Isaiah's throne room gave rise to early Jewish mysticism. The Song of Songs has been interpreted as an allegory of the soul's union with God by mystics from Origen to Bernard of Clairvaux.

The Septuagint and Manuscript Tradition

The Septuagint (LXX), a Greek translation produced in Alexandria from the third century BCE, was the Bible of the earliest Christians and the New Testament authors. The Masoretic Text, the authoritative Hebrew text established by Jewish scholars (Masoretes) in the sixth to tenth centuries CE, forms the basis of most modern Old Testament translations. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947 onwards) provided manuscripts a thousand years older than the Masoretic tradition, confirming its remarkable fidelity while also revealing textual variants of historical interest.
