King James Version

The Authorised Version of 1611 — a masterwork of English prose and sacred translation, commissioned by King James I and crafted by the finest biblical scholars of the Jacobean age.

← Christianity

Origins and Commission

The King James Version (KJV) was commissioned in 1604 at the Hampton Court Conference, called by King James I of England (James VI of Scotland) in response to Puritan requests for a new Bible translation. Fifty-four scholars — drawn from the Church of England's finest biblical humanists — were organised into six companies, each assigned a portion of the scriptures. The translation drew on William Tyndale's foundational work, the Geneva Bible, the Bishops' Bible, and the original Hebrew and Greek texts. After three years of collective labour and review, the completed Bible was published in 1611.

Literary Grandeur

The KJV is widely regarded as one of the supreme achievements of English prose. Its translators, steeped in the cadences of late Tudor and early Jacobean English, produced a text of extraordinary rhythmic power, syntactic clarity, and imaginative depth. Phrases coined or crystallised by the KJV — "the salt of the earth," "a labour of love," "the eleventh hour," "by the skin of my teeth," "the valley of the shadow of death" — have become permanent features of the English language, testament to the translation's penetrating influence on literature, law, politics, music, and everyday speech.

Translation Principles

The translators worked from the Textus Receptus (the received Greek New Testament text compiled by Erasmus and later editions) for the New Testament, and from the Masoretic Text for the Old Testament, supplemented by the Septuagint and Vulgate. The fifteen translation rules issued at the outset instructed the scholars to follow the Bishops' Bible as the base text while making revisions as the original languages required, to preserve ecclesiastical terminology familiar to English congregations, and to work collectively so that no individual's idiosyncrasy would dominate the final text.

Influence on English Culture

The KJV shaped English-speaking culture more profoundly than perhaps any other single text. John Milton, John Bunyan, William Blake, Herman Melville, Toni Morrison, and countless other writers drew on its imagery and cadences. Abraham Lincoln's second inaugural address is saturated with its language. The Psalms and Isaiah of the KJV gave hymnody, oratorio, and choral music — from Handel's Messiah to the spirituals of enslaved African Americans — their scriptural vocabulary. For three centuries it was the Bible of the English-speaking Protestant world.

The Apocrypha in the KJV

The 1611 edition included the Apocrypha — the deuterocanonical books — placed between the Old and New Testaments, following the practice of pre-Reformation Bibles. These books were gradually removed from most Protestant printings of the KJV during the nineteenth century, particularly as the British and Foreign Bible Society (founded 1804) declined to fund their inclusion in mission Bibles. Early editions with the Apocrypha remain valuable for scholars and for liturgical traditions that continue to use these texts.

Legacy and Continued Use

Despite the proliferation of modern translations — the Revised Standard Version (1952), the New International Version (1978), the English Standard Version (2001) — the KJV retains a devoted readership and a unique liturgical authority in many communities. The King James Only movement regards the 1611 text as uniquely inspired and authoritative. For many others, the KJV is valued as a devotional classic and a cultural treasure, read not despite but because of its archaic beauty — a voice from an age when the English language was at one of its most luminous peaks.
