Invocation & Evocation

The twin arts of ceremonial magic — the disciplined calling of divine powers into the self through invocation, and the summoning of spiritual intelligences to visible or sensible appearance through evocation.

What Are Invocation & Evocation?

The theoretical distinction between invocation and evocation is deceptively simple but practically consequential. Invocation (from the Latin invocare, to call upon, to call into) refers to the deliberate calling of a divine power, spirit, or aspect of consciousness into the practitioner — into the ritual space of the self, the body, and the psyche. The practitioner who successfully invokes a divine power does not merely contact it externally but becomes, temporarily and deliberately, a vehicle for its expression. The Golden Dawn ritual of the Bornless One is an invocation: the practitioner identifies themselves utterly with the divine power being called. Evocation (from the Latin evocare, to call out, to call forth) refers to the calling of a spirit, daemon, or intelligence to appear before the practitioner — within a triangle of art, in a crystal, or in some other defined locus — as an entity that is distinctly other than the practitioner. The practitioner remains themselves; the evoked being appears as separate.

The roots of both practices run deep into the most ancient strata of religious history. The Greek Magical Papyri (PGM) — a corpus of magical texts from Graeco-Roman Egypt, dating from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE and preserved primarily in the great Leiden and London papyri — contain some of the earliest and most detailed magical working texts in the Western tradition. They include elaborate invocations of solar and lunar powers in which the practitioner identifies themselves with the deity being invoked ("I am you, and you are I; your name is my name..."), as well as evocations of divine assistants, protective daemons, and spirits of the dead. The PGM synthesis of Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, and early Christian divine names and ritual procedures represents the common magical substrate from which both the medieval grimoire tradition and the Renaissance esoteric revival drew.

The grimoire tradition — the body of magical textbooks produced in medieval and Renaissance Europe — systematised evocation in particular into a comprehensive and highly formalised practice. The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis), probably composed in the 14th or 15th century though claiming a much older provenance, provides detailed instructions for the preparation of the practitioner through fasting, prayer, and ritual purification, the construction and consecration of magical instruments (wand, sword, lamen, seal), the drawing of protective circles and triangles, and the procedure for compelling spirits to appear and fulfil the practitioner's requests. The Lemegeton (or Lesser Key of Solomon), particularly its first part the Goetia, lists 72 spirits or daemons with their specific seals, powers, and the manner of their conjuration and dismissal.

The Golden Dawn system of magic, developed by S.L. MacGregor Mathers, William Wynn Westcott, and William Robert Woodman in the 1880s, represented a comprehensive synthesis and systematisation of both invocatory and evocatory practice within a Kabbalistic framework. The Neophyte ceremony was an initiatory invocation of the divine light; the higher-grade rituals systematically invoked the divine powers associated with each Sephira of the Tree of Life. The technique of "Rising on the Planes" — an invocatory practice in which the practitioner progressively identifies with and ascends through the divine powers of the Kabbalistic hierarchy — remains one of the most influential innovations in Western magical practice. Evocation was relegated to the more advanced and specialised work of the Second Order.

Aleister Crowley's system of Thelemic magic, set out in Liber ABA (Magick in Theory and Practice) and numerous other works, placed invocation at the absolute centre of magical practice. His key technical instruction — "Inflame thyself in praying" — points to the essential quality that distinguishes effective invocation from mere recitation: a total emotional, imaginative, and intellectual identification with the power being invoked. Crowley's Liber Samekh, a version of the Bornless Ritual from the PGM, is intended as an invocation of the practitioner's own Holy Guardian Angel (HGA) — the higher self or divine genius that Thelemic magic regards as the primary object of magical attainment. The Knowledge and Conversation of the Holy Guardian Angel, achieved through sustained invocatory practice, is for Crowley the equivalent of the Hermetic gnosis, the Kabbalistic devekut, and the Sufi fana.

The psychological versus ontological debate about what invocation and evocation actually contact has been vigorous throughout the 20th and 21st centuries. The psychological interpretation — advanced by Dion Fortune, Israel Regardie, and in a more systematic form by the Jungian analysis of magic — holds that invoked and evoked beings are aspects of the practitioner's own psyche: archetypes of the collective unconscious, sub-personalities, or aspects of the deep self that are contacted through the ritual framework. Evoking a spirit "to visible appearance" is, on this view, a vivid form of active imagination. The ontological interpretation holds that the beings contacted in evocation are genuinely independent entities with their own intelligence, will, and existence, not reducible to any aspect of the practitioner's mind. Both views have sophisticated defenders, and the debate has never been resolved — which is perhaps as it should be.

Contemporary ceremonial magic, represented by practitioners and authors such as Peter Carroll (chaos magic), Jason Miller (Strategic Sorcery), and Jake Stratton-Kent (the Geosophia and True Grimoire tradition), has moved away from the grand synthesising systems of the 19th century toward a more pragmatic and eclectic approach. Chaos magic, in particular, explicitly suspends the ontological question — treating magical entities as "belief tools" whose reality-status is deliberately left undetermined — and focuses instead on the practical question of whether the working produces the intended result. This pragmatic turn has been criticised by traditionalists as philosophically shallow but defended as honest about the genuine uncertainty of the ontological question.

The Lesser Banishing Ritual of the Pentagram (LBRP), devised in the Golden Dawn and made public by Aleister Crowley, is the most widely practised invocatory-protective ritual in the modern Western tradition. Combining Kabbalistic divine names, archangelic invocations, and geometric symbolism, it is typically used both to open and close magical operations and as a daily practice for the maintenance of psychic clarity and protection. Its influence on contemporary magical practice can hardly be overstated — virtually every Western ceremonial magical tradition uses it or a variant of it as the foundation of daily practice.

Key Concepts & Systems

📜

The Greek Magical Papyri

2nd BCE – 5th CE

The PGM represent the most complete surviving body of practical magical instruction from the ancient Mediterranean world — a rich synthesis of Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, and early Christian magical traditions. They contain invocations of solar and planetary powers, spells for protection, love, and cursing, and sophisticated techniques of divine identification. Hans Dieter Betz's critical edition and translation (1986) made these texts fully accessible to modern scholars and practitioners. The PGM are the primary historical source for understanding what actual ancient magical practice looked like.

📖

The Grimoire Tradition

Medieval to Renaissance Europe

The grimoires — magical handbooks of the medieval and Renaissance periods — systematised evocatory practice into elaborate procedural rituals involving consecrated tools, protective circles, binding oaths, and hierarchical classifications of spirits. The Key of Solomon, Lemegeton, Abramelin the Mage, and Arbatel of Magic represent different currents within this tradition. Joseph Peterson and Jake Stratton-Kent have produced modern critical editions that illuminate these texts' complex manuscript histories and their relationship to classical, Jewish, and Arabic magical sources.

🌟

Golden Dawn Invocations

The Synthesised System

The Golden Dawn system integrated Kabbalistic divine names, astrological correspondences, Enochian angelic language, and Egyptian godform assumption into a comprehensive framework for both invocation and evocation. The technique of "assuming a godform" — mentally inhabiting the form and consciousness of an Egyptian deity — is a form of invocation unique to the Golden Dawn synthesis. Israel Regardie's publication of the complete Golden Dawn documents (1937–40) made this system available to the public and shaped virtually all subsequent ceremonial magic in the English-speaking world.

⭐

The Lesser Banishing Ritual

Foundation of Daily Practice

The LBRP is the most widely practised ritual of the modern Western magical tradition — a compact working combining Kabbalistic affirmation (the Qabalistic Cross), pentagram geometry, and the invocation of the four archangels (Raphael, Gabriel, Michael, Uriel) in the four quarters. Originally a Grade 0 exercise in the Golden Dawn, it has become the standard foundation of Western magical practice. Its daily use is understood to maintain psychic clarity, establish sacred space, and gradually develop the practitioner's relationship with the divine powers it invokes.

👁️

Goetic Evocation

The Art of Calling Forth

The evocation of the 72 spirits of the Goetia — the first book of the Lemegeton — represents the most formalised evocatory system in the Western tradition. Each spirit is associated with a specific seal, rank (king, duke, earl, president, marquis, knight), powers, and number of legions of spirits commanded. The evocatory procedure involves extensive preparation, a protective circle inscribed with divine names, a triangle of art for the spirit's appearance, and a brass vessel or black mirror as its locus. Whether understood as genuine independent entities or as aspects of the magician's unconscious, the Goetic spirits have a consistent phenomenology across centuries of recorded working.

🔮

Crowley & Thelemic Practice

Invocation of the HGA

Crowley's technical definition of magic as "the science and art of causing change to occur in conformity with Will" places invocation of the Holy Guardian Angel — the divine genius of the individual — at the summit of magical attainment. His Liber Samekh is a step-by-step invocatory ritual of extraordinary intensity, designed to be practised repeatedly until the Knowledge and Conversation of the HGA is achieved. This operation — equivalent to what Abramelin calls the "Sacred Magic" — is Crowley's version of the universal mystical goal: the direct experience of one's own divine nature.

Between Self and Other

The philosophical question at the heart of invocation and evocation — whether the beings contacted are genuinely external entities or aspects of the practitioner's own psyche — is, upon reflection, less decisive than it initially appears. Consider: even if one accepts the psychological interpretation, the beings contacted in magical ritual are aspects of the deep psyche — the Jungian unconscious, the collective memory, the archetypal layer of the mind that transcends the individual. These are not simply "made up" by the practitioner's conscious imagination. They are accessed through a structured ritual technology that bypasses ordinary consciousness and allows a deeper layer of mind to become operative. Whether one calls this layer "the unconscious" or "the spirit world" may be more a question of cultural framing than of genuine ontological distinction.

The contemporary chaos magic framework cuts through this debate with elegant pragmatism. Peter Carroll and the practitioners associated with the Illuminates of Thanateros (IOT) treat magical entities as belief tools: structured patterns of intentionality that can be assigned any degree of ontological reality the practitioner finds useful for the work at hand. The magician might approach one working with full conviction in the independent reality of the evoked being, and another with detached psychological awareness, and choose between these orientations based on what produces the most effective result. This approach has the virtue of intellectual honesty about genuine uncertainty while retaining the practical power of full engagement.

What the centuries-long practice of invocation and evocation consistently produces — regardless of ontological interpretation — is a structured engagement with forces, patterns, and intelligences that consistently exceed the practitioner's ordinary self-concept. Whether these are "external" or "internal," they exhibit a stability, a consistency, and a capacity for surprise that distinguishes them from the products of ordinary imagination. The spirit evoked into the triangle responds in ways the practitioner did not anticipate. The invoked divine power produces effects in the practitioner's life that confirm its presence beyond the scope of self-suggestion. These consistent phenomenological features do not resolve the ontological question, but they establish that something real is occurring — something that deserves the careful, disciplined attention that the great ceremonial traditions have always brought to it.
