Inuit Angakkuq Traditions

The shaman-healers of the Arctic — their extraordinary cosmology of Inua and Sila, the central descent to Sedna, trance journeys between worlds, and one of the most remarkably preserved shamanic traditions on Earth.

Introduction

The Angakkuq (plural: Angakkulit) are the shaman-healers of the Inuit peoples — the indigenous inhabitants of the Arctic regions spanning Alaska, Canada, Greenland, and northeastern Siberia. The Inuit have developed one of the most remarkable civilisations in human history: a complete culture adapted to flourish in the harshest environment on Earth, with a spiritual tradition of extraordinary sophistication shaped by millennia of intimate relationship with ice, sea, and sky.

The geographical spread of Inuit peoples is vast — from the Yupik of western Alaska and Siberia, through the Inupiat of northern Alaska, the many Inuit nations of Canada's Arctic (Inuvialuit, Kivalliq, Qikiqtaani), to the Kalaallit of Greenland. Despite this spread of several thousand kilometres, the fundamental features of Inuit cosmology — the concept of Inua, the role of the Angakkuq, the centrality of Sedna, the drum trance — are remarkably consistent across all these groups, suggesting a shared spiritual tradition of great antiquity.

The concept of Inua — meaning "its person," "its spirit," or "its inner form" — is the foundational ontological principle of Inuit cosmology. Every living thing, and many apparently non-living things, possesses an Inua: every animal, every plant, every feature of the landscape, every weather phenomenon. The Inua is the animating intelligence and inner personhood of a thing — the particular way in which it participates in the consciousness that pervades the Arctic cosmos. The hunter who kills a seal must honour the seal's Inua through specific ritual actions; failure to do so will cause the seals to withdraw from the hunting grounds, offended by the disrespect.

Sedna — known across Inuit traditions as Nunavgak, Arnaqquassaaq, Sanna, Nerrivik, and Uinigumassuittuq — is the most cosmologically important figure in Inuit spiritual tradition. A young woman thrown from a boat whose severed fingers became the seals, walruses, and whales upon which Inuit survival depends, she rules the ocean floor. When her hair becomes tangled with the transgressions of the people above, the sea animals hide. The central shamanic journey of the Angakkuq — the descent to comb Sedna's hair — is one of the most extraordinarily preserved spiritual narratives in the world.

The Angakkuq's World

👁️

Inua (The Spirit Within)

The animating consciousness dwelling within all things — the "inner person" of animals, plants, landscape features, and natural phenomena. The Angakkuq navigates a world entirely alive and personal: not a mechanical environment but a community of persons, each with their own will and capacity for relationship.

🌊

Sedna (The Sea Mother)

Ruler of the undersea world and mother of the sea animals. Her body is the ocean; the seals, walruses, and whales are her children, formed from her severed fingers. Her emotional state determines the availability of game. The Angakkuq's periodic descent to comb Sedna's hair is the most important service they perform for their community's survival.

🐻

Tuurngait (Spirit Helpers)

The helping spirits of the Angakkuq — acquired during initiation and through ongoing spiritual work. Tuurngait may be spirits of deceased animals (particularly bears), spirits of land or sea, or human spirits. The Angakkuq's power is inseparable from their tuurngait relationships; without these helpers they cannot journey to the spirit world, heal illness, or influence the forces affecting their community.

💨

Sila (Cosmic Intelligence)

One of the most philosophically profound concepts in Inuit thought — the cosmic intelligence pervading the atmosphere and the entire world. Sila is simultaneously the Inuit word for "weather," "consciousness," "intelligence," and "the universe." For the Angakkuq, attunement to Sila is both a meteorological skill and a spiritual capacity: the ability to perceive the mind of the cosmos as it manifests in wind and weather.

🥁

Qilaut (The Drum)

The central tool of Angakkuq practice — the vehicle through which trance is induced and the spirit journey undertaken. The traditional Inuit frame drum is played to induce qaumaniq, the shaman's inner light. The sound of the drum is understood as the heartbeat of the universe, the voice of Sila itself. The associated drumming songs open the gateway between ordinary reality and the spirit world.

🦴

Tupilak (Spirit Constructs)

A spirit being constructed from bones, sinew, and organic matter, animated by the practitioner's tuurngait and directed toward a specific purpose. The creation of a tupilak was highly dangerous: if the target was spiritually more powerful than the sender, the tupilak could be turned back to destroy its creator. Today tupilak figures are carved in bone and ivory as art objects, their original power transformed into aesthetic form.

The Descent to Sedna

The journey to Sedna is the most distinctive and cosmologically central shamanic journey of the Inuit Angakkuq — a narrative of breathtaking power that is also a functional spiritual technology, performed regularly on behalf of the community. When hunting has failed and the people face starvation, the cause is understood as spiritual: the sea animals are hiding because Sedna's hair has become tangled with the accumulated transgressions of the community. Food taboos broken, animals improperly treated after the kill, social prohibitions violated — all these produce a kind of spiritual pollution that tangles itself in Sedna's hair, distressing her and causing her to withdraw her children from the hunters.

To remedy this, the Angakkuq enters trance through drumming, singing, and breath control, then descends through the layers of the sea to Sedna's dwelling at the ocean floor. This is a genuine journey through hostile territory — the underwater world is full of dangerous spirits, and the Angakkuq must navigate past them using their tuurngait helpers. Arriving at Sedna's dwelling, the Angakkuq finds her hair in terrible tangles — filthy, matted, impossible to separate. The Angakkuq approaches with respect, acknowledges the community's transgressions, and gently begins to comb and plait Sedna's hair. As the hair is combed, Sedna is soothed; as she is soothed, the sea animals hidden in the tangles are released, swimming upward to the hunting grounds above.

The Angakkuq returns to the community gathering place and reports what was found — which transgressions had caused the tangling, which violations the community needed to confess and correct. This public confession was an essential part of the ceremony. The Descent to Sedna is one of the most remarkably preserved shamanic narratives in the world — documented in essentially identical form by explorers and anthropologists across the entire Arctic range of the Inuit, from Alaska to Greenland, in communities with no known prior contact with each other. It stands as perhaps the most universal spiritual narrative preserved within a single cultural tradition.

Trance and Initiation

The calling to become an Angakkuq is typically not chosen but received — often through a "shamanic illness": a period of severe physical or psychological crisis that represents the spirit world's forceful selection of the individual for healing work. The illness is understood as the spirits breaking down the ordinary self in order to rebuild it as a vessel fit for spiritual work. Recovery from this illness — during which the young Angakkuq may have extraordinary visions, hear voices, and undergo experiences indistinguishable from severe mental illness to outside observers — is itself the first initiation, the first demonstration that this individual has the capacity to die and be reborn.

The formal initiation of an Angakkuq involves the experience of being "dismembered" by the helping spirits — a complete dissolution of the ordinary self, perceived as being torn apart, stripped of flesh, reduced to bones, and then reconstructed. Knud Rasmussen's accounts of Angakkuq initiation collected in the early 20th century describe with startling precision how initiates perceived themselves being taken apart and rebuilt — sometimes by a bear stripping the flesh from the bones; sometimes by ancestral Angakkuq; sometimes by mysterious light-beings who enter the body and reside in the bones as permanently installed helping spirits.

The ongoing practice of the Angakkuq centres on using the qilaut (drum) and katajjaq (drumming songs) to enter qaumaniq — the state of inner illumination described by Rasmussen's informant Aua as "a light that enabled me to see in the dark, both literally and metaphorically." In this state, the Angakkuq can see into the bodies of sick people to diagnose illness, perceive the location of game at great distances, communicate with tuurngait, and travel to the spirit world. This capacity for luminous perception — cultivated through years of practice, ceremony, and the maintenance of helping spirit relationships — is the central spiritual gift of the Angakkuq and the foundation of their service to their community.

Recommended Reading

📖

Knud Rasmussen (1879–1933)

Across Arctic America (1927) and the ten-volume Report of the Fifth Thule Expedition (1921–1924). The Danish-Greenlandic explorer whose interviews with Angakkuq across the entire Arctic — including his famous conversations with the shaman Aua — produced the richest documentation of traditional Inuit shamanic practice available anywhere. His account of Aua describing qaumaniq (the shaman's light) is one of the most remarkable documents in the history of shamanism.

📚

Larry Merkur

Becoming Half Hidden: Shamanism and Initiation among the Inuit (1985). The most thorough scholarly analysis of Inuit shamanic initiation practices, drawing on the full range of ethnographic literature to analyse the cosmological structure of Angakkuq calling, initiation, and practice. Essential academic reading for serious students of Arctic shamanism.

🌿

Ann Fienup-Riordan

Boundaries and Passages: Rule and Ritual in Yup'ik Eskimo Oral Tradition (1994). A meticulous and respectful study of Yup'ik (Western Alaskan) cosmology, spiritual practice, and oral tradition, written in close collaboration with Yup'ik elders. Among the most culturally sensitive and intellectually rigorous treatments of any Arctic indigenous tradition available in English.

❄️

Knut Hammarberg & Comparative Sources

The broader corpus of comparative Arctic cosmological research documenting Sedna-variant narratives collected across the Inuit world, demonstrating both the consistency of the core narrative and the remarkable cultural creativity with which different Inuit nations have elaborated it. Essential for understanding the cosmological significance of this central Inuit spiritual narrative and its place within the wider tradition of Arctic shamanism.
