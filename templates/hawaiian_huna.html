Hawaiian Huna

The esoteric philosophy of ancient Hawai'i — the three selves, mana, the High Self, and the hidden science behind traditional Hawaiian healing, prayer, and spiritual practice preserved by the Kahuna across generations.

The Hidden Science of the Kahuna

The word "Huna" (pronounced HOO-nah) means "secret" or "hidden" in the Hawaiian language — the concealed, the esoteric, the inner dimension of things. In the context of Hawaiian spiritual tradition, Huna refers to the body of esoteric knowledge preserved and transmitted by the Kahuna — the "keepers of the secret," Hawai'i's traditional spiritual and practical experts whose knowledge encompassed healing, navigation, prayer, prophecy, canoe-building, astronomy, and much more. The Hawaiian spiritual tradition is one of the most sophisticated indigenous esoteric systems in the Pacific, and one that contains striking parallels to other esoteric traditions worldwide, particularly in its system of the three selves and its understanding of spiritual energy.

Before exploring the system, an important historical note is necessary. "Huna" as a formalised, named system was largely codified and systematised by Max Freedom Long (1890–1971), an American schoolteacher who lived in Hawai'i and became fascinated by the Kahuna traditions after witnessing events he could not explain through conventional Western frameworks. Long's books — particularly Recovering the Ancient Magic (1936) and The Secret Science Behind Miracles (1948) — drew on the earlier work of William Tufts Brigham (curator of the Bernice Pauahi Bishop Museum) and on his own analysis of the Hawaiian language to reconstruct what he believed was the original Kahuna esoteric system. Long's work is influential and contains genuine insights, but it is important to recognise that his synthesis is a 20th-century reconstruction rather than an unbroken traditional transmission.

The three selves — Unihipili, Uhane, and Aumakua — form the central framework of Huna psychology and spirituality. In the Huna system as systematised by Long, the human being is understood as consisting of three distinct selves or aspects of consciousness, each with its own nature, capacities, and role. The Unihipili (the low self, subconscious, or body self) is the part of us that is connected to the body, holds all memories and emotions, communicates with the physical world, and serves as the channel through which our prayers and intentions reach the High Self. The Uhane (the middle self, the conscious/talking self) is the everyday rational mind — the aspect of us that thinks, plans, reasons, and makes decisions. The Aumakua (the High Self, superconscious, or utterly trustworthy parental self) is the divine aspect of the individual — the higher consciousness that is in direct contact with spiritual reality and guides the individual soul across multiple lifetimes.

The correspondence between the Huna three selves and the Western psychological categories of unconscious, conscious, and superconscious is striking, and has led many researchers and practitioners to see in Huna a confirmation of the universality of these distinctions. The Unihipili corresponds closely to what Freud called the unconscious and what modern psychology calls the somatic unconscious; the Uhane is the everyday waking mind; the Aumakua is the Jungian Self, the Higher Self of Theosophy, or the Holy Guardian Angel of Western esotericism. Whether these correspondences reveal a shared universal truth about the structure of human consciousness, or whether they reflect Long's own syncretistic framework, remains an open question — but the practical utility of the three-selves model as a working framework for healing and spiritual development is attested by thousands of practitioners worldwide.

The Three Selves

🌱

Unihipili (The Low Self / Body Self)

The subconscious self in Huna — the aspect of the human being connected to the body, the emotions, and the memory. The Unihipili is not "lower" in the sense of inferior but in the sense of being the foundation, the root, the earth aspect of the human being. It stores all memories, including those from past lives; it controls bodily functions; it generates and holds mana (spiritual power); and crucially, it is the intermediary through which the Uhane (conscious self) communicates with the Aumakua (High Self). All prayers, if they are to be effective, must be properly communicated to and carried by the Unihipili. A blocked or uncooperative Unihipili — often due to unresolved emotional issues or "complexes" — can prevent the effective passage of prayer to the High Self.

🧠

Uhane (The Middle Self / Talking Self)

The conscious, rational, ego-level self — the "I" of ordinary waking experience. The Uhane thinks, plans, communicates in language, and makes decisions. In Huna, the Uhane is responsible for the spiritual development of the Unihipili — for educating it, healing its emotional wounds, maintaining the conditions for effective prayer, and directing the overall spiritual life of the individual. Without a clear, intentional Uhane, the Unihipili is directionless and the Aumakua unreachable. Meditation, self-reflection, and conscious self-development are the primary practices through which the Uhane fulfils its role as guide and educator of the body self.

✨

Aumakua (The High Self / Superconscious)

The divine aspect of the individual — utterly trustworthy, all-loving, and in direct communication with the spiritual dimension of reality. The Aumakua is simultaneously personal (your own High Self, the divine aspect of your own being) and universal (all Aumakua are connected as expressions of the divine). In Huna practice, the primary spiritual goal is to establish a clear, open channel of communication with the Aumakua through the purified Unihipili. The Aumakua responds to prayers carried on the aka cord (the silver thread of mana and intention) by the Unihipili, and can act in the world to bring about the desired conditions — but only if the path is clear.

Mana and Healing

Mana is the Hawaiian concept of spiritual power — the invisible, life-giving force that flows through all things and that can be accumulated, directed, and transmitted by skilled practitioners. In Polynesian cultures across the Pacific (mana appears in different forms in Māori, Tongan, Samoan, and Fijian traditions), mana is not merely symbolic but a real energetic reality — the spiritual substance of the universe itself. A person of great mana radiates an almost tangible presence; their words carry power; their touch can heal. In Huna psychology, mana is generated primarily by the Unihipili through breathing and physical vitality, stored in the aka body (the subtle body), and can be accumulated through proper breathing, prayer, and spiritual practice for use in healing, ceremony, and petition to the High Self.

Ho'oponopono — the Hawaiian practice of reconciliation, forgiveness, and the correction of errors — is one of the most beautiful and practically powerful of all Hawaiian spiritual technologies, and one that has had remarkable global reach in recent decades largely through the work of Morrnah Nalamaku Simeona (1913–1992) and her student Dr Ihaleakalā Hew Len. In its traditional form, Ho'oponopono was a group process facilitated by a healer (Kahuna) or family elder in which community members came together to resolve conflict, forgive injuries, and restore right relationship. The four key phrases of modern Ho'oponopono — "I love you, I'm sorry, please forgive me, thank you" — represent an act of total responsibility for one's experience: the recognition that everything in one's reality is a reflection of one's own inner state, and that healing any problem requires healing one's relationship to it from within.

Lomilomi — traditional Hawaiian massage — is one of the most profound forms of sacred healing art in the Pacific. More than a physical massage technique, lomilomi is understood as a spiritual healing practice: the practitioner works not only on the physical body but on the entire energetic being of the recipient, using long, flowing strokes that move in rhythmic patterns inspired by the movement of ocean waves. Traditional lomilomi was transmitted within families and carried specific ceremonial prayers and intentions. The practitioner prepares themselves through prayer and the accumulation of mana before working, and the session is understood as a ceremony of love, forgiveness, and restoration — a full-body ho'oponopono enacted through touch.

The Kahuna

The word "Kahuna" means "keeper of the secret" or "expert in the secret" — from ka (the) + huna (secret, concealed). Kahuna were the specialists and experts of traditional Hawaiian society, holding knowledge in virtually every domain of life — and in each domain, the knowledge had both a practical and a sacred dimension. The Kahuna were not a single category but many: the Kahuna lapa'au (healing Kahuna) specialised in herbal medicine, diagnosis, and treatment; the Kahuna nui (high Kahuna) performed the most important religious ceremonies; the Kahuna kalai wa'a (canoe-building Kahuna) held the sacred knowledge of canoe construction including the prayers and ceremonies required to make a vessel seaworthy; the Kahuna kilokilo (prophet) could read signs in the stars, clouds, and natural phenomena to forecast events; the Kahuna ho'opāpā were masters of philosophical debate. Each speciality represented decades of apprenticeship and the transmission of a complete, sacred body of knowledge.

The transmission of Kahuna knowledge operated within family lineages (hālau) — the knowledge was passed from parent to child, uncle to nephew, elder to apprentice, within specific family groups who held the rights and responsibilities associated with particular domains of practice. This family-based transmission system meant that Kahuna knowledge was extraordinarily stable and precise across generations; it also meant that the near-destruction of Hawaiian culture after Western contact in 1778 and the conversion campaigns of Christian missionaries beginning in the 1820s was catastrophically effective. Within a generation or two, the political, social, and religious structures that supported Kahuna transmission were destroyed, and much knowledge that had taken millennia to accumulate was lost.

The near-destruction of Hawaiian spiritual traditions is one of the great cultural tragedies of the colonial era. The arrival of Captain James Cook in 1778 initiated a process of demographic collapse (disease killed approximately 90% of the native Hawaiian population within a century), political transformation (the abolition of the kapu system in 1819), and cultural suppression that devastated the institutions through which Kahuna knowledge was transmitted. The Hawaiian language was banned in schools from 1896 to 1986. The hula — which is not merely a dance but an encoded archive of Hawaiian cosmological knowledge — was suppressed by missionaries who considered it immoral. The remarkable renaissance of Hawaiian culture that has been underway since the 1970s — re-establishing the language, the hula, the traditional navigation (Hōkūle'a), and aspects of Kahuna knowledge — is one of the most inspiring stories of cultural resilience and recovery in the world.

Recommended Reading

Max Freedom Long

1890–1971 · Huna Researcher & Author

The foundational texts of the modern Huna movement, in which Long presents his reconstruction of the ancient Kahuna system based on linguistic analysis of the Hawaiian language and accounts of Kahuna practices. Long's synthesis drew on decades of research and correspondence with practitioners and scholars worldwide. Essential for understanding the Huna framework, though best read with awareness that Long's synthesis is a 20th-century reconstruction rather than an unbroken traditional transmission.

Key works: The Secret Science Behind Miracles (1948); Growing Into Light (1955)

Serge Kahili King

Contemporary · Hawaiian Shamanism Author

One of the most prolific and accessible writers on Hawaiian shamanism and Huna, King claims lineage from a Hawaiian Kahuna. His work extends the Huna framework into practical applications for modern life, particularly through the Huna philosophy of the seven principles (Ike, Kala, Makia, Manawa, Aloha, Mana, Pono). King's accessible style and emphasis on practical application have made his books some of the most widely read introductions to Hawaiian spiritual philosophy.

Key works: Urban Shaman (1990); Kahuna Healing (1983)

Mary Kawena Pukui

1895–1986 · Hawaiian Cultural Practitioner & Scholar

The definitive insider account of traditional Hawaiian cultural and spiritual practices by one of the most important Hawaiian cultural practitioners of the 20th century. Pukui was also the primary author of the Hawaiian-English Dictionary — the most comprehensive dictionary of the Hawaiian language — and her knowledge of traditional Hawaiian culture was encyclopaedic. Her Nana I Ke Kumu (Look to the Source) remains the most authoritative study of Hawaiian beliefs, practices, and values from an insider perspective.

Key works: Nana I Ke Kumu (Look to the Source) (1972, 2 vols., with E.S.C. Handy)

Nana Veary

1908–1994 · Hawaiian Spiritual Teacher

A deeply personal account of Kahuna spiritual practices and Ho'oponopono by a Hawaiian woman who received traditional teachings from her aunt and who taught these practices widely on the mainland United States. Veary's warm, accessible style makes this one of the most personally resonant introductions to authentic Hawaiian spiritual practice available. Her account of receiving and transmitting traditional Hawaiian healing knowledge stands as a testament to the resilience of indigenous wisdom in the face of cultural suppression.

Key works: Change We Must: My Spiritual Journey (1989)
